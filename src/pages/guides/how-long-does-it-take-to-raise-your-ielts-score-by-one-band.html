How long does it take to raise your IELTS score by one band?

Usually longer than people hope. A full band means a real change in your English, which takes months, but your overall score can move faster if you find where you are losing marks for reasons that have nothing to do with your level.

What the research actually says

IELTS has published a plain answer, and it's a sobering one. Drawing on two studies of students in preparation courses, it says it takes students three months to improve by up to half a band. It also gives an example of one student who studied around 60 hours a week for three months and improved by a full band.

The same article lists what made the biggest difference: starting below 5.5, studying more hours, and being more motivated. Anxiety and not enjoying the course worked the other way.

It also warns against trusting online success stories. Someone who "went from 6 to 7.5 in three weeks" may already have had the English and only needed to learn the test format. Or they may be quoting one skill, not their overall band.

Source: IELTS, How long will it take to get the IELTS band score I need?. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

Why one band is harder than it sounds

A band isn't a number of correct answers. In Writing and Speaking, the examiner compares you with a description of what each band looks like. To move from 6 to 7 in Speaking, for example, you need to speak at length without noticeable effort, use less common vocabulary with some awareness of style, and produce frequent error-free sentences. That is a different kind of speaker, not the same speaker with fewer mistakes.

Source: IELTS, Speaking band descriptors. Check them before your exam in case anything has changed.

That's why working professionals, who might manage five or six hours a week, should think in terms of months for a full band in a skill, not weeks. Anyone promising otherwise is guessing.

Where your score can move faster

Here's the useful part. Many candidates I teach are already capable of a higher score than they get. They lose marks to things that are quick to fix:

  • Writing Task 2 under length or off topic. Task 2 counts twice as much as Task 1, so an essay that answers a slightly different question costs a lot.
  • Memorised Speaking answers. Examiners notice, and the answer stops counting as evidence of your real level. See why memorised answers lose marks.
  • Careless Listening and Reading answers. Spelling counts, and so do plurals and word limits.
  • Running out of time in Reading or Writing because of no plan.

Fixing these doesn't change your English. It changes how much of your English the examiner gets to see. That's the gap that can close in a few weeks.

The rounding effect

Your overall band is the average of the four skills, rounded to the nearest half band. An average ending in .25 rounds up to the next half band, and one ending in .75 rounds up to the next whole band.

So "raise my score by one band" might mean half a band each in two skills, not a full band in all four. If your listening and reading are strong, lifting your two weakest skills by half a band may be enough. That's a more realistic target in a few months.

Be careful, though: if your target is a minimum in every skill, as it is for UK visas, rounding won't save a weak skill. My guide to IELTS for UK visas explains that rule.

How to plan your own timeline

  1. Take a full, timed practice test now. Not a quiz, a real paper. You need four separate scores, not a feeling.

  2. Split the gap by skill. Write down how far each skill is from your target. The biggest gap sets your timeline.

  3. Sort each gap into "test technique" or "English". Lost marks from timing, misreading or spelling are technique. Not understanding a recording or running out of words to explain an idea is English.

  4. Book the exam with honest room. If the gap is mostly technique, a month of focused work may do it. If it is mostly English, think in months, and keep a regular weekly routine rather than a last-minute cram.

  5. Retest halfway. If nothing has moved after a few weeks, change what you're doing, not just how much.

A quick self-check

Ask yourself one question: when you lose marks, is it because you didn't know, or because you didn't show what you knew? Students often assume it's the first. When we look at their practice papers together, a surprising amount turns out to be the second, and that's the part you can fix quickly.
