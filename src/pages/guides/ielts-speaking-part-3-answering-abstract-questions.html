IELTS Speaking Part 3: answering abstract questions

Part 3 moves from your life to society in general, and that's where many candidates freeze. The fix is to stop looking for a clever answer and use a simple shape: opinion, reason, example, and a second side.

How Part 3 works

Part 3 is the last part of the Speaking test and lasts 4–5 minutes. The examiner leads a two-way discussion about issues connected to your Part 2 topic, "in a more general and abstract way". If your Part 2 card was about a teacher who influenced you, Part 3 might ask how education is changing, or whether teachers are valued enough.

The British Council describes the focus as justifying opinions, comparing perspectives and speculating. Those three skills are exactly what to practise.

You're marked on the same four criteria as the rest of the test, equally weighted: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation.

Sources: IELTS Speaking format on ielts.org and the British Council's Speaking test format page. Check them before your exam in case anything has changed.

Why Part 3 feels harder

In Parts 1 and 2 you talk about yourself, which you know. In Part 3 you're asked about things you may never have thought about: "Why do you think some people prefer to live alone?" Students often go silent because they're looking for the right answer.

There isn't one. The examiner is assessing your English, not your knowledge. A simple, reasonable opinion explained clearly scores better than a brilliant idea you can't express.

A four-step answer shape

  1. Opinion: answer the question directly in your first sentence.

  2. Reason: explain why.

  3. Example: something specific, from your country, your work or people you know.

  4. Other side or future: a contrast, a condition or a prediction.

Question: "Do you think people read less than they used to?"

Answer: "I think they probably read just as much, but they read differently. Most people now read on their phones, so it's more news articles and messages than novels. For example, on the train to work almost everyone is reading something, but very few of them are holding a book. That said, I think audiobooks might bring long-form reading back for some people, because you can listen while you're doing something else."

That's about 30 to 40 seconds. Long enough to show your range, short enough to stay natural. The examiner will follow up anyway.

Phrases for the three Part 3 skills

Giving and justifying opinions

  • "I'd say that… mainly because…"
  • "It depends to some extent on…"
  • "I'm not completely sure, but my feeling is that…"

Comparing

  • "Compared with my parents' generation, people now…"
  • "In cities it's quite different, whereas in rural areas…"
  • "Older people tend to…, while younger people are more likely to…"

Speculating about the future

  • "I imagine that in twenty years…"
  • "It's likely that… although it's hard to say for certain."
  • "If this trend continues, we might see…"

Notice the softening language: "I'd say", "tend to", "it's likely". Natural English speakers hedge a lot when discussing big questions, and it shows grammatical range without sounding forced.

Moving from personal to general

A common Part 3 problem is answering an abstract question with a personal story only. "Why do people move to big cities?" "I moved to Warsaw because…" That's a start, but the question is about people in general.

Use your own experience as the example, not the whole answer: "Mostly for work, I think. Big cities simply have more opportunities, especially in sectors like finance or tech. That was certainly true for me when I moved…"

When you don't know what to say

Buy a few seconds in English, then give an honest, simple answer:

  • "That's a difficult one. I've never really thought about it, but I suppose…"
  • "I'm not an expert on this, but from what I've seen…"
  • "Do you mean in my country, or in general?"

Asking a clarifying question is fine and natural. Long silence isn't.

Common mistakes

  • One-sentence answers. "Yes, I think so." Then silence. Always add the reason and example.
  • Over-long speeches. Part 3 is a discussion, not another Part 2. Two minutes on one question leaves no room for the conversation.
  • Memorised material. Prepared paragraphs are obvious in a discussion. See why memorised answers lose marks.
  • Using "I think" for everything. Vary it: "In my view", "I'd argue", "It seems to me that".

How to practise

After each Part 2 practice card, write three "why", "how" and "what will happen" questions about the general topic, and answer them out loud with the four-step shape. Record yourself and check two things: did you answer in your first sentence, and did you give an example? For Part 2 practice itself, see how to talk for two minutes.
