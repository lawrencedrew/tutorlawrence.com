Words that are pronounced differently from how they look

English spelling is a poor guide to pronunciation. These are the everyday and work words I hear mispronounced most often, with simple respellings so you can say them the British way today.

How to read the respellings

Capital letters show the stressed syllable. "uh" is the short, relaxed sound in the middle of "about". One important point for standard British English: an r after a vowel is usually silent. So "UR" in a respelling means the vowel in "turn", "OR" means the vowel in "more", and "AH" means the vowel in "car", all with no r sound.

Swallowed syllables

Some words lose a whole syllable in normal speech. Saying every letter makes them sound careful and foreign.

"Business" is the one that matters most at work. If you say "BIZ-ee-ness", people will understand you, but it is one of the most noticeable non-native features in a meeting.

Vowels that surprise people

  • busy: BIZ-ee. The u sounds like the i in "is".
  • women: WIM-in. The o sounds like the i in "him". (Woman, singular, is WUH-mun, with the oo of "good".)
  • says and said: SEZ and SED. Not "SAYZ".
  • colleague: KOL-eeg. Two syllables. The ending sounds like "league".
  • determine: di-TUR-min. The end rhymes with "pin", not "mine".
  • recipe: RESS-uh-pee. Three syllables, not "re-SIPE".
  • chaos: KAY-oss. The ch sounds like k.
  • archive: AH-kive. Again ch = k, and the end rhymes with "five".
  • suite (of software or rooms): SWEET. Same sound as "sweet".
  • queue: KYOO. The "ueue" is all one sound.
  • warn: sounds exactly like "worn". Not like "barn".
  • iron: EYE-un. No r sound in the middle.
  • colonel: KUR-nul. Sounds the same as "kernel".

The -ed ending: the rule nobody teaches properly

This is the most useful thing on the page, because it affects hundreds of verbs. The past tense -ed has three sounds, and it only adds a syllable after a t or d sound.

  • After t or d: an extra syllable, "id". needed (NEE-did), started (STAH-tid), decided.
  • After p, k, s, sh, ch, f: a "t" sound, no extra syllable. worked (WURKT), stopped (STOPT), finished (FIN-isht), asked (AHSKT).
  • After everything else: a "d" sound, no extra syllable. planned (PLAND), called (KAWLD), changed, organised.

The common mistake is "work-ED" and "plan-ED" with an extra syllable. It slows you down and can even confuse listeners about the word.

British place names you will meet at work

  • Leicester: LESS-tuh. Worcester: WUSS-tuh. Gloucester: GLOSS-tuh.
  • Greenwich: GREN-itch or GREN-idge. Norwich: NORR-idge, with a short o as in "hot". The w is silent in both.
  • Thames: TEMZ. The h is silent.
  • Edinburgh: ED-in-bruh. Not "-burg".

Words with two accepted versions

Not every variation is a mistake. "Schedule" is traditionally SHED-yool in Britain, but SKED-yool is also common now. "Often" can be OF-un or OF-tun. "Either" can start with EE or EYE. Choose one and use it confidently. If you work with British colleagues, the first options here are the safe choice.

How to fix a mispronunciation for good

Knowing the right version is not enough, because your mouth will go back to the old habit under pressure. Pick three words from this page that you use at work. Say each one correctly in a short sentence, ten times, today: "I've sent you the receipt", "The business case is ready", "I'll check with my colleague". Then do it again tomorrow. After about a week, the new version wins. Stress matters just as much as sounds, so read my guide to word stress next.
