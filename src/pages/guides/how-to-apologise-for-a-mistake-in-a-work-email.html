How to apologise for a mistake in a work email

A good apology email says sorry once, takes responsibility, explains what you're doing to fix it, and then stops. The fix matters more to the reader than the sorry.

The structure that works

  1. Apologise and name the mistake. "I'm sorry for sending the wrong file this morning."

  2. Take responsibility. "That was my error." No long story about why.

  3. Give the fix. "I've attached the correct version."

  4. Say what happens next. "I've added an extra check before files go out, so it won't happen again."

  5. Close normally. "Thanks for your patience." / "Let me know if you have any questions."

For a small mistake, those five parts can fit into three sentences. For a serious one, each part might be its own paragraph. The order stays the same.

Choosing the right level of apology

SituationPhrase
Tiny slip with a colleagueSorry, wrong attachment. Here's the right one.
Small mistake with a clientApologies for the confusion. The correct figure is €4,200.
Mistake that caused extra workI'm sorry for the extra work this caused your team.
Serious mistake, formalPlease accept our sincere apologies for the error in last month's invoice.
A delay on your sideSorry for the slow reply. / Apologies for the delay in getting back to you.

Match the size of the apology to the size of the problem. A formal "sincere apologies" for a typo sounds odd, and a quick "sorry!" for a lost shipment sounds careless.

Phrases that sound wrong, and better versions

AvoidWrite instead
I apologise for the inconvenience caused to you.I'm sorry for the trouble this caused.
Sorry if you were confused.Sorry, my last email wasn't clear.
Mistakes were made.I made a mistake with the dates.
I am so so sorry, I feel terrible, it will never happen again.I'm sorry about this. I've changed how I check these, so it shouldn't happen again.
We excuse for the delay.We apologise for the delay.

"Sorry if you were confused" puts the problem on the reader. "Mistakes were made" hides who made them. Both sound defensive to English readers. The last row is a translation trap: in English you "apologise for" something; "excuse" is used differently, as in "Excuse me".

A full example to a client

"Subject: Correction: invoice 2291

Dear Ms Lindqvist,

I'm sorry, but the invoice we sent you on Monday had the wrong unit price for the March delivery. That was our error.

I've attached a corrected invoice, which replaces the original. The total is now €3,860 instead of €4,120, and the payment date stays the same.

We've also added a second check on all invoices before they're sent, so this shouldn't happen again.

Thank you for pointing it out so quickly, and please let me know if you have any questions.

Kind regards,
Daniel"

Notice the subject line. It says "Correction", which tells the reader immediately what kind of email this is, and lets them find it later.

A full example to a colleague

"Hi Ines,

Sorry, I gave you the wrong deadline yesterday. The client needs the slides by Wednesday, not Thursday. My mistake.

I know that's a day less than you planned for. I can take the last three slides if that helps. Just let me know.

Thanks,
Rafa"

Short, honest, and it offers help. That offer of help does more for the relationship than any amount of apologising.

Apologising when it wasn't your fault

Sometimes you need to apologise for your company, even if you personally did nothing wrong. Use "we" and focus on the fix:

  • I'm sorry to hear about the problem with your delivery.
  • I'm sorry you've had this experience.
  • I understand how frustrating this must be.

These phrases show sympathy without admitting legal responsibility, which matters if the situation is still being investigated. Then move straight to what you're doing about it.

How long should it be?

Shorter than you think. Students often write a full paragraph of apology before getting to the fix, because in their language a longer apology feels more respectful. In English, a long apology can make a small mistake look big, and it delays the information the reader actually needs. One sentence of sorry, then action.

A good test: if you deleted the apology sentence, would the rest of the email still be useful? It should be. The apology is a courtesy; the fix is the content.

The word that does most of the work

In lessons I often suggest one replacement: change "I apologise for any inconvenience" to "Thank you for your patience". The first looks backwards at the problem.
