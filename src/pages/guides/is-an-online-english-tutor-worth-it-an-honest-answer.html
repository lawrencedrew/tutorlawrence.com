Is an online English tutor worth it? An honest answer

Sometimes yes, sometimes no. A tutor is worth it when you need feedback, a deadline or speaking practice you can't get elsewhere. It isn't worth it if you can't attend regularly or if what you really need is more input, which you can get for free.

First, my bias

I'm an online English tutor, so I have an obvious interest in your answer. I'll try to be fair anyway, because a student who shouldn't be in lessons doesn't benefit from them, and that's no good for either of us. Here is how I'd think about it if I were in your position.

What a tutor gives you that free resources don't

There is more free English material online than anyone could use in a lifetime. Grammar explanations, vocabulary lists, podcasts, videos, practice tests. Information is not the problem. What free resources can't easily give you is:

  • Feedback on what you produce. An app can tell you a gap-fill answer is wrong. It can't hear that you stress "development" on the wrong syllable, or tell you your polite email sounds slightly cold to a British reader.
  • Speaking time with someone who adapts to you. For many working adults, a lesson is the only time they speak English at length without stakes.
  • Prioritising. A good tutor tells you which of your twenty problems matter and which you can ignore. That saves more time than any study technique.
  • Accountability. A booked lesson on Thursday is harder to skip than a plan to study "this week".
  • Material from your real life. Your presentation next week, your interview, the email you are nervous about sending.

When a tutor is probably worth it

  • You have a specific goal with a date: an exam, an interview, a new role in English, a presentation.
  • You are stuck at a level and don't know why. This is very common around B2, where your fixed mistakes need someone else to spot them. See how to keep improving after B2.
  • Your main need is speaking, and you have nobody to practise with.
  • You are confident reading and writing at work but freeze in meetings.
  • Your time is limited, so you need every hour to be focused on your weaknesses.

When it probably isn't

  • You can't attend regularly. One lesson a month, with nothing in between, rarely produces much. You are better off with a consistent free routine.
  • You won't do anything between lessons. A weekly hour is a small part of a week. Lessons work best as the steering wheel, not the engine. If you can't give at least a little time outside lessons, progress will be slow whoever teaches you.
  • Your main need is more exposure. If you understand English but want to get more comfortable with it, more reading and listening may be enough. My guide to learning with podcasts when you work full time is a good free starting point.
  • The money would cause you stress. Language learning is a long process. If paying for lessons means cutting something important, it is not a good trade, and the alternatives below are real options.

A combination often works best: free input every day, a speaking partner, and lessons when you have a specific goal or need to break through a plateau.

How to tell if a tutor is right for you

Not every tutor will suit you, and a poor fit is the most common reason lessons don't feel worth it. In a first or trial lesson, look for these signs:

  1. They ask about your goals and your work before deciding what to teach.

  2. They correct you. Friendly chat with no feedback feels pleasant but teaches little. Ask how and when they correct mistakes.

  3. You speak more than they do. In a speaking-focused lesson, that is how it should be.

  4. You leave with something concrete: phrases, corrections, a clear next step.

  5. They are honest about time. Be careful with anyone who promises a level jump in a few weeks.

Give it four to six lessons before you judge. The first couple are mostly about the tutor learning how you speak. After that, you should be able to name specific things you do better.

The short answer

A tutor is worth it when you use them for what they are uniquely good at: feedback, focus and speaking practice tied to a real goal. It isn't worth it as a replacement for your own effort, or when the cost would outweigh the benefit. Be honest about which situation you are in.
