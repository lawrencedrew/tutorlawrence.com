Small talk before meetings: what to say and what to avoid

Small talk isn't about the topic. It's a two-minute signal that you're friendly and relaxed. You need three safe topics, a few follow-up questions, and the habit of giving slightly longer answers than feels natural.

What small talk is actually for

Students often tell me small talk is harder than the meeting itself. That makes sense. The meeting has an agenda. Small talk has no script, and it can feel pointless: why talk about the weather with someone you'll never see in person?

But in British and international workplaces, those first minutes do a real job. They warm the room up, they show you're approachable, and they make the harder conversation later go more smoothly. Nobody remembers what you said. They remember whether it felt easy.

Three safe topics that always work

  • The week or the weekend. "How's your week going?" "Did you get up to much at the weekend?"
  • Where the other person is. "Are you in the office today or working from home?" "What's the weather like over there?"
  • Work, lightly. "Is it busy at the moment?" "How did the conference go?"

Yes, the weather really does work, especially on video calls between countries. "It's been raining all week here. What's it like in Kraków?" is a perfectly good opener, and it gives the other person an easy answer.

The rule that changes everything: answer plus one

The biggest small-talk problem I see isn't vocabulary. It's short answers. Someone asks "How was your weekend?" and you say "Good, thanks." Then silence. You answered correctly, but you closed the conversation.

Instead, give your answer plus one extra detail, and if you can, a question back.

Closes the conversationKeeps it going
Good, thanks.Good, thanks. We finally went hiking, the weather was perfect. How about you?
Busy.Pretty busy. We're launching a new product next month, so it's all hands on deck. Is it busy your end?
Yes, from home.Yes, from home today. It's nice to skip the commute. Are you in the office?

Answer, detail, question. Practise that pattern and you'll never be stuck in silence again.

Useful follow-up questions

When the other person tells you something, pick it up with a short question:

  • "Oh really? How was it?"
  • "That sounds great. Where did you go?"
  • "Sounds busy. Is it always like that this time of year?"
  • "Oh no. Is everything OK now?"

Short reaction words also help a lot: "Oh nice", "Really?", "Lovely", "That's annoying". They show you're listening, and they buy you a second to think of your next line.

What to avoid

  • Salary and money. "How much do you earn?" or "How much did your house cost?" are off limits in British culture, even with colleagues you know well.
  • Politics and religion. Even if you're sure the other person agrees with you, it's not small-talk material.
  • Age, weight, appearance and family plans. "You look tired" or "When are you having children?" will make people uncomfortable.
  • Complaining about the company or other colleagues. You don't know who's listening, especially on a recorded call.
  • Deep questions too quickly. "Are you happy in your job?" is fine over a drink with a friend, not two minutes before a project meeting.

Moving from small talk to business

The move into the meeting should feel smooth, not sudden. Use a linking phrase:

  • "Anyway, shall we get started?"
  • "Right, I'm conscious of time, so let's dive in."
  • "Great, well, it looks like everyone's here."

"Anyway" is the most common one, and it does a lot of work in British English. It means "that was nice, and now we're changing topic". If you're the one leading the meeting, there are more phrases for each stage in how to run a meeting in English.

A short example

A: "Morning, Laura. How's your week going?"

B: "Not bad, thanks. A bit hectic. We've had auditors in since Monday. How about you?"

A: "Oh, that sounds intense. It's quieter here, luckily. Are they there all week?"

B: "Until Thursday, thankfully."

A: "Well, nearly there, then. Anyway, I think Tom's joined, so shall we start?"

That's under a minute, and it does everything small talk needs to do.

A tip from lessons: prepare one thing to say

Before an important meeting, think of one small, true thing from your week that you could mention: a trip, a film, a new café near the office, the weather. You probably won't need it. But knowing it's there takes the panic away, and you'll relax enough to respond naturally to whatever the other person says.
