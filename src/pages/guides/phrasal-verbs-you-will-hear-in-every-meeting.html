Phrasal verbs you will hear in every meeting

Native speakers rarely say "postpone", "investigate" or "summarise" in a meeting. They say "put off", "look into" and "sum up". If you know about twenty phrasal verbs well, meetings become much easier to follow.

Why phrasal verbs are a problem in meetings

In writing, most people use the formal verb. In speech, the phrasal verb takes over. So you can read every report in your company easily and still lose the thread when your manager says, "Let's park that and circle back to it once we've ironed out the pricing." This guide covers the ones I hear most in real meetings, in the order a meeting usually goes.

Starting the meeting

  • kick off = start. Shall we kick off? / Let's kick off with the sales figures.
  • go over = review, check through. Let's go over the action points from last week.
  • run through = explain or review quickly. Can you run through the timeline for us?

Discussing the topics

  • bring up = mention a topic. I'd like to bring up the budget.
  • come up = be mentioned, or happen unexpectedly. The question of hiring came up last week. / Something's come up, so I need to leave early.
  • point out = draw attention to. Marta pointed out that the figures don't match.
  • look into = investigate. I'll look into it and let you know.
  • figure out / work out = find the answer to. We need to work out why sales dropped. ("Work out" is more British.)
  • iron out = solve small problems or details. There are a few issues to iron out before we sign.
  • rule out = decide something is not possible. We haven't ruled out a second supplier.
  • back up = support a person or an argument. The data backs this up. (Also: save a copy of files.)

Managing time and order

  • move on (to) = go to the next point. Let's move on to item three.
  • put off = postpone. We've put off the launch until spring.
  • hold off (on) = wait before doing something. Let's hold off on the announcement until we hear from legal.
  • park (not a phrasal verb, but used like one) = leave a topic for later. Can we park that for now?
  • circle back (to) = return to a topic later. Let's circle back to this next week.
  • get back to = reply later. I'll get back to you on that by Friday.

Closing the meeting

  • sum up = summarise. So, to sum up, we're going with option B.
  • wrap up = finish. Let's wrap up there, we're nearly out of time.
  • follow up (on) = take further action after the meeting. I'll follow up with an email. / Can you follow up on the quote?
  • sign off (on) = officially approve. We need the finance director to sign off on this.
  • drop off (on calls) = leave. Sorry, I need to drop off in five minutes.

The ones with two meanings

A few phrasal verbs change meaning completely depending on context. These are the ones that cause real misunderstandings.

Push back

  • We've pushed back the deadline to March. = delayed it.
  • The client pushed back on the price. = resisted, objected.

The clue is "on": "push back on something" is almost always the objection meaning.

Put forward and bring forward

  • She put forward a proposal. = suggested it.
  • We've brought the meeting forward to Tuesday. = made it earlier.

People often confuse "bring forward" (earlier) with "push back" (later). If in doubt, ask: "Sorry, is that earlier or later?". Native speakers mix these up sometimes too.

Take over

  • Tom will take over the project when I'm on leave. = become responsible for.
  • The company was taken over last year. = bought by another company.

Grammar: where does the object go?

Many phrasal verbs are separable. With a noun, the object can go before or after the particle. With a pronoun (it, them, this), it must go in the middle.

WrongRight
Can you bring up it at the meeting?Can you bring it up at the meeting?
We need to put off it.We need to put it off.
Let's go it over.Let's go over it.

The last one is the trap: "go over", "look into" and "come up" are not separable. A dictionary will show you which is which, usually with "sth" placed in the middle or at the end.

Which ones should you use yourself?

You need to understand all of these. You don't need to use all of them. My advice is to choose five that fit your job and start using them in calls this week. Good first choices: look into, get back to, go over, follow up and move on. They're safe, neutral, and immediately make your English sound more natural.

Leave the more idiomatic ones, like "circle back" and "park", until you hear your own colleagues using them. Some teams use them constantly, others find them irritating corporate jargon.
