ECL C1 speaking topics and sample answers

ECL publishes its topic list, so you know the areas you can be asked about. Below are the C1 topics, three model answers I've written to show the level, and a way to prepare that doesn't involve memorising speeches.

Where the topics come from

ECL's topic list is cumulative. At C1 you can be asked about anything on the A2, B1 and B2 lists, plus topics added at C1. The 20 topic areas run from the individual and family through work, media and health to public life, environmental protection and globalisation.

How the topics appear in the exam

In the guided conversation you and your partner discuss a topic based on four bullet points. In the monologue you talk about a montage of four pictures. You get no preparation time before either, so you can't plan on the day. You can only prepare the topics in advance.

Source: ECL exam format page and exam regulations. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

The tasks below are my own practice tasks in the exam style, not official ECL papers, and the answers are models I've written to show C1 language.

Sample 1: generational differences at work

Practice task (guided conversation): Discuss generational differences in the workplace. Talk about: communication styles; attitudes to working hours; what each generation can learn from the other; how managers should respond.

A: "I'd start with communication, because that's where I notice it most. Younger colleagues seem to prefer a quick message, whereas older ones would rather pick up the phone. Have you seen that?"
B: "Definitely. Although I'm not sure it's purely about age. It's more about what people got used to early in their careers."
A: "That's a fair point. And I think it ties in with working hours. People who started out with a fixed nine-to-five tend to see presence as commitment."
B: "Whereas younger staff judge themselves by results. Honestly, I think both sides have a point. Shall we talk about what they could learn from each other?"

Why it works: short turns, questions to the partner, a polite challenge ("I'm not sure it's purely about…"), and an explicit move to the next bullet point.

Sample 2: consumer society

Practice task (picture monologue): Four pictures: a crowded shopping centre during sales, a second-hand market, a delivery van outside a block of flats, a repair café.

Model answer, about 90 seconds:

"What links these pictures is the way we buy and use things. I'd put them into two groups. The shopping centre and the delivery van show consumption at its most convenient, and probably its most wasteful. The market and the repair café show the opposite trend: people trying to make things last.

The woman in the delivery picture looks as though she's receiving her third parcel this week. That's speculation, of course, but it reflects something real. Online shopping has made buying almost effortless, and when something is effortless, we rarely stop to ask whether we need it.

Personally, I'm encouraged by places like repair cafés, though I suspect they appeal to a fairly small group. For a real change, I think you'd need manufacturers to make products that can actually be repaired. Otherwise, the responsibility falls entirely on consumers, which doesn't strike me as fair."

Why it works: it groups the pictures instead of listing them, speculates ("looks as though", "I suspect"), gives an opinion with a reason, and widens the topic to who is responsible.

Sample 3: trust in experts

Practice question: Why do some people distrust scientific experts?

Model answer: "I think there are a few overlapping reasons. One is that science changes its mind, which is actually its strength, but to the public it can look like inconsistency. Another is the sheer volume of information online, where a confident non-expert often sounds more convincing than a careful scientist. And to be fair, there have been cases where official advice was poorly communicated. So rather than blaming people, I'd say experts need to be clearer about what they know and what they don't."

Why it works: a clear structure ("one is… another is…"), a concession ("to be fair"), and a balanced conclusion.

How to prepare each topic in 20 minutes

  1. Collect 8–10 topic phrases you can say comfortably: "a growing divide", "to bridge the gap", "it comes down to".

  2. Decide one opinion and one reason for it.

  3. Prepare one example from your own life or work.

  4. Say it aloud for one minute, recorded. Listen back once. Don't write it out.

Writing out full answers leads to memorising, and memorised answers sound recited and rarely fit the exact bullet points you're given. That's true in every speaking exam, as I explain in why memorised answers lose marks.
