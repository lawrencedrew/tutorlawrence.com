ECL listening: how to prepare in five weeks

Five weeks is enough to make a real difference to your ECL listening score, if you practise the way the exam works: two recordings, each heard twice, with short, precise answers.

What the listening module involves

B2C1
Time25–30 min30–35 min
Recordings2, 800–1,000 words in total2, 1,200–1,500 words in total
Items2020
Recording typesConversations, reports, news, interviews, radio reports, short lecturesConversations, reports, lectures, news, interviews, radio reports

The questions are multiple choice with three options and short answers. Each recording is played twice. You get up to two minutes to read the questions before the first playing, a pause after it, and time after the second playing to finish. Each item is worth 1.25 points, there's no partial credit, and since August 2026 you need 60% in the module to pass it, which is 12 of the 20 items.

One practical rule: if you arrive after the listening module has started, you won't be let in.

Week 1: find your starting point

Do one full listening section from an official ECL sample test, timed, with the pauses as in the exam. Mark it, then sort every mistake into one of three groups:

  • Didn't hear it: speed, accent or connected speech.
  • Heard it, misunderstood it: vocabulary or a trap.
  • Understood it, wrote it badly: too many words, wrong form, spelling.

This takes ten minutes and tells you how to spend the next four weeks. Each group needs a different fix.

Daily habit from week 1: 15 minutes of English audio on C1 topics such as work, the media, the environment or public life. News programmes and interview podcasts are ideal because they match the exam's recording types.

Week 2: train the two listenings

The exam gives you two chances. Use them differently.

  1. Before the first listening: underline the key word in each question and predict the kind of answer: a number, a reason, a person, a place.

  2. First listening: follow the overall structure and note answers lightly. Don't stop to agonise over one item, or you'll miss the next two.

  3. In the pause: see which items are still empty. That is your target list for the second listening.

  4. Second listening: confirm what you have and listen hard for the gaps.

Practise this exact routine with every recording this week, including your daily listening. Pause the audio yourself to copy the exam's rhythm.

Week 3: the traps

Most wrong answers I see in listening come from a few recurring patterns. Learn to hear them:

  • Self-correction: "We met on Tuesday… no, sorry, it was Wednesday." The answer is the correction.
  • Two speakers, two opinions: the question asks what the interviewer thinks, and you write the guest's view.
  • Mentioned but rejected: "We thought about moving to Vienna, but in the end…" Vienna is a distractor.
  • Paraphrase: the question says "cost", the speaker says "it wasn't cheap". You must listen for meaning, not matching words.

Exercise: take a five-minute interview with a transcript. Listen once, answer your own three questions, then find in the transcript the exact words that gave each answer. You'll see how rarely the question uses the same words as the speaker.

Week 4: short answers and speed

Short answers should be a few words, and anything extra or wrong in them costs the point. Write the minimum:

Too muchEnough
"He says it was because of the bad weather in the mountains""bad weather"
"About 300, maybe 350 people"The number the speaker settles on

This week, increase difficulty: listen to lectures and radio discussions at normal speed, and do one full sample listening section under exam conditions. If your accent recognition is the weak point, mix British, American and other accents in your daily listening.

Week 5: exam conditions only

  • Do the remaining official sample listening section, timed, with no replays except the second listening.
  • Practise transferring answers to an answer sheet in black or blue ink. Only the answer sheet is marked.
  • Reduce new material in the last three days. Re-listen to recordings you already know, so your ear stays sharp without fresh frustration.

A realistic weekly time budget

For a working professional this plan needs about 15 minutes a day of everyday listening plus one 45-minute focused session a week. That is about three hours a week. It's enough, as long as the focused session uses exam-style questions rather than just "listening more".

If you're preparing all four modules, the listening work fits into the wider five-week ECL C1 study plan. The ECL C1 format guide covers the rest of the exam.
