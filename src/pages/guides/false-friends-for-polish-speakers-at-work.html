False friends for Polish speakers at work

Polish has plenty of words borrowed from Latin, French and English that look familiar to an English speaker. Some of them mean something quite different. Here are the ones that cause real confusion at work, and what to say instead.

Why false friends are worth your attention

Most mistakes make a sentence sound slightly wrong, so the listener works out what you meant. A false friend makes a sentence sound completely right, with a different meaning. "Is the offer still actual?" is understandable. "We discussed my pension" during a pay review is not: your manager will think you're talking about retirement.

The ones I correct most often

Aktualny, aktualnie and actually

"Actually, I work in procurement" doesn't mean "at the moment". To a British listener, "actually" signals a correction: "in fact, despite what you think". British people use it all the time to disagree gently: "Actually, I think the meeting's at three." So say currently or at the moment.

"Aktualny" has a second work meaning that catches people out. Czy oferta jest aktualna? is "Is the offer still valid?" or "Is the position still open?", never "still actual". And an up-to-date price list is the current price list or the latest price list.

Ewentualnie and eventually

"Eventually" means something definitely happens, after a long time or a lot of effort: The client eventually paid. Polish speakers often use it to offer an alternative, which confuses the listener. Compare:

  • Możemy się spotkać we wtorek, ewentualnie w środę. → We can meet on Tuesday, or Wednesday if that's better.
  • Ewentualnie mogę to wysłać jutro. → If need be, I can send it tomorrow.

Useful replacements: alternatively, or, if necessary, if need be, possibly.

Pensja and pension

"My pension is paid on the 10th" sounds like you have retired. Say salary (fixed yearly or monthly pay, for office jobs) or pay / wages (more general, often weekly or hourly). A pay rise is a pay rise in British English and a raise in American English.

Protokół and protocol

"Who is writing the protocol?" will confuse a British colleague. A written record of a meeting is the minutes, always plural: Could you take the minutes today? I'll circulate the minutes by Friday. In a less formal team, people just say notes or a summary. "Protocol" is for official procedures, diplomatic etiquette or IT: safety protocol, network protocol.

Ewidencja and evidence

"Evidence" means proof, usually in a dispute or investigation. Ewidencja czasu pracy is time records or timesheets. Ewidencja środków trwałych is a fixed asset register. "We keep evidence of hours" sounds like you're preparing for a court case.

Ekspertyza and expertise

"Expertise" is what a person has: She has a lot of expertise in tax law. It is uncountable, so never "an expertise". The document an expert writes is an expert report or an expert opinion.

Konsekwentny and consequent

"He is very consequent" isn't English. Say consistent (he always applies the same standard) or he follows things through. While we're here, a person who is konkretny is best described as direct, to the point or no-nonsense. "A concrete person" sounds very strange.

Outside the office

  • ordynarny = vulgar, coarse. "Ordinary" means normal, everyday.
  • sympatyczny = nice, friendly, likeable. "Sympathetic" means understanding about someone's problem.
  • recepta = prescription. A "receipt" is what you get after paying (paragon), and a "recipe" is for cooking (przepis).
  • akademik = hall of residence or student halls. An "academic" is a university researcher or lecturer.
  • prezerwatywa = condom. A "preservative" keeps food fresh (konserwant).

How to stop making them

Search your sent emails for "actually", "eventually", "actual" and "protocol". These are the ones I see most often in work emails, and once they become a habit they appear every week. Then write one replacement sentence for each about your real job, for example "At the moment, I'm responsible for supplier contracts", and say it aloud until it comes out first.
