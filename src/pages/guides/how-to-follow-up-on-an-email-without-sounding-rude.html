How to follow up on an email without sounding rude

A good follow-up assumes the other person is busy, not ignoring you, and makes it easy for them to reply. Here are the phrases for a first, second and final reminder.

Why follow-ups go wrong

Most rude-sounding follow-ups aren't rude in the writer's language. They become rude when they are translated word for word. "I am still waiting for your answer" is a neutral fact in many languages. In English, it sounds like you're tapping your foot.

The British approach is to take the blame off the reader. You pretend, politely, that the email might have got lost, or that you're just checking. Everyone knows what you mean, and nobody loses face.

Phrases to avoid, and what to write instead

Sounds impatientSounds polite
I am still waiting for your answer.I just wanted to check whether you've had a chance to look at this.
As I wrote in my previous email…Just bringing this back to the top of your inbox.
Why haven't you replied?I know things are busy at the moment.
Please answer urgently.Could you let me know by Thursday? I need to confirm with the client on Friday.
Reminder!!!Quick follow-up: venue booking for 12 May

One phrase deserves a special warning: "As per my last email". Native speakers often read it as passive-aggressive. It means "I already told you this". Avoid it unless you want that effect.

The first follow-up

Keep it short. Reply in the same thread, so the original email is underneath, and add two or three lines on top.

"Hi Paolo,

Just following up on my email below about the training dates. Could you let me know which week works best for your team? I'd like to book the trainer by the end of next week.

Thanks,
Eva"

Notice what it does: it points to the original, repeats the request in one sentence, and gives a reason for the timing. It doesn't apologise for writing and doesn't complain.

Other good openings for a first follow-up:

  • I just wanted to check in on the email below.
  • I'm not sure if this reached you, so I'm resending it.
  • Sorry to chase, but…
  • Just a gentle reminder about…

"Sorry to chase" is a very British phrase. You're not really sorry, and the reader knows that. It just softens the reminder.

The second follow-up

By the second reminder, make the reply even easier. Offer options, shorten the request, or suggest a call.

"Hi Paolo,

Sorry to chase again. To make it easier, would the week of 9 June or 16 June work better? A one-word answer is fine.

If it's simpler to discuss, I'm happy to have a quick call this week.

Thanks,
Eva"

Giving two options is the most useful trick I teach for follow-ups. A busy person can answer a choice in ten seconds. An open question waits until they have time to think, which may be never.

The final follow-up

If you still have no reply, it's fine to be clear about what happens next. The tone stays calm.

"Hi Paolo,

I haven't heard back about the training dates, so I'll go ahead and book the week of 16 June. If that doesn't work for your team, just let me know by Wednesday and I'll change it.

Best regards,
Eva"

This is the "default action" follow-up. You decide, explain the decision, and give the other person a chance to stop it. It's polite, and it moves things forward. If the reason for the delay is on your side instead, see how to explain a delay or a problem to your manager.

When to send it

There's no fixed rule, but these are sensible defaults:

  • Urgent, same-day matters: follow up after a few hours, or pick up the phone.
  • Normal work requests: two or three working days.
  • Sales or first-contact emails: about a week.

If you gave a deadline in the first email, wait until the deadline has passed. Following up before it sounds like you didn't trust the person.

Subject lines for follow-ups

Usually you just reply in the same thread and keep the subject. If you start a new email, make the subject specific:

  • Following up: training dates for June
  • Quick reminder: signed NDA needed
  • Still need: your feedback on the draft

Avoid capital letters, "URGENT" and exclamation marks. They make the reader defensive before they open the email.

A small detail that changes the tone

Look at your first line. If it starts with "I", as in "I sent you an email last week", the email is about you and your frustration. If it starts with the task, as in "Just following up on the training dates", it's about the work. It's a small change, and it shifts the whole tone of the email.
