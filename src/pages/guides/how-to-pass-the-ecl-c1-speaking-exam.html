How to pass the ECL C1 speaking exam

The ECL C1 speaking exam rewards real conversation, not prepared speeches. Talk to your partner, not at them, and build your picture talk around a comparison and an opinion. Here is how to do both.

How the speaking module works

You take the test with another candidate, and it lasts about 18–20 minutes for a pair. There are three parts:

  1. Warm-up. A few questions about you. It isn't marked, but it is your chance to settle your voice.

  2. Guided conversation. You and your partner discuss a topic based on four bullet points.

  3. Monologue. Each of you talks alone about a montage of four pictures.

You don't get preparation time before the conversation or the monologue. You start speaking as soon as you see the task. Two examiners score you independently on five criteria, each worth up to 5 points:

  • formal accuracy (grammar)
  • oral accuracy: pronunciation, intonation and fluency
  • vocabulary range
  • style: whether your language fits the situation
  • communicative effectiveness: whether you actually complete the task

Since August 2026 you need at least 60% in each module to pass it, so 15 of the 25 speaking points. Speaking is now a module in its own right: pass it and you get a certificate for it, even if another module goes badly.

The guided conversation: make it a real conversation

This is where I see the most points lost by good speakers. Each person gives a neat one-minute opinion, the other person waits, then gives theirs. That is two monologues, and it doesn't show interaction.

Aim for short turns, around 20–40 seconds, and hand over to your partner each time. Use phrases like these:

To do thisSay this
Invite your partner in"What's your take on that?" / "Is it the same where you work?"
Build on their point"That's a good point, and I'd add that…"
Disagree politely"I see it slightly differently, actually."
Move to the next bullet point"Shall we move on to the question of…?"
Summarise"So we both seem to think…, although we disagree about…"

Here is how that sounds with a C1 topic from the official list, working from home versus the office:

A: "For me, the biggest advantage of working from home is the time I save. I used to spend over an hour a day commuting. Is it similar for you?"
B: "Partly, yes. But I'd say the downside is that you lose the informal conversations, the kind of thing you'd pick up at the coffee machine."
A: "That's true, although I think it depends on the team. Some teams are very good at keeping in touch online."
B: "Maybe. Shall we move on to how it affects younger employees? I think they lose out the most."

Notice that nobody speaks for long, each person reacts to the last thing said, and the move to the next bullet point is done naturally. Cover all four bullet points: that is part of completing the task.

The picture monologue: describe less, compare and judge more

At C1, simply listing what is in each picture ("In the first picture there is a man…") is a waste of your time. The examiners want to hear you organise ideas. A structure that works for almost any montage:

  1. Name the theme in one sentence. "All four pictures show different ways people deal with stress."

  2. Group and compare. "The two on the left are about solving it actively, while the others seem more about escaping it."

  3. Speculate. "She looks as if she's just finished a long shift. I'd imagine…"

  4. Give your opinion with a reason. "Personally, I think the first approach works better in the long run, because…"

  5. Link it to wider society. "More generally, I think employers have started to take this more seriously."

Speculation language is a quick way to show C1 range: "might well be", "it's hard to say for sure, but…", "judging by…", "she can't be enjoying it much".

If you get stuck

Everyone loses a word in an exam. At C1, how you recover matters. Don't stop and search silently. Paraphrase: "it's a kind of… tool you use to…". The official writing descriptors at C1 even mention circumlocution, getting round a missing word, as a way to overcome gaps in vocabulary, and the same skill helps you in speaking.

Useful fillers that sound natural rather than nervous:

  • "That's a tricky one. Let me think."
  • "How can I put it…"
  • "What I mean is…"

Prepare the topics, not the answers

ECL publishes its topic list. At C1 you can be asked about everything from the lower levels plus C1 topics such as consumer society, intercultural communication, the impact of influencers, artificial intelligence at work, and the life situation of older people.

Don't memorise answers. Memorised speeches sound flat, and they rarely fit the exact bullet points you get. Instead, for each topic area, prepare:

  • 8–10 topic words you can actually pronounce, such as "overconsumption" or "algorithm"
  • one opinion you can defend
  • one personal example

Practise with a partner

The paired conversation is a skill you can't build alone. Practise with a classmate, a colleague, or a teacher playing your partner. Record yourselves for ten minutes, then listen for two things: who talked more, and how many times you asked each other a question. If the answer to the second is "almost never", that is your first thing to fix.
