Negotiation phrases in English

Good negotiation English is polite, conditional and very precise. The most useful grammar you can learn is "if you…, we could…". Here are the phrases for each stage, and the mistakes that cost people money.

The tone to aim for

English-language negotiations, especially with British counterparts, tend to sound friendly on the surface while being quite firm underneath. People rarely say "No" directly. They say "That would be difficult for us", and they mean no. If you speak very directly, you may come across as aggressive. If you miss the soft signals, you may think a door is open when it's closed.

So two skills matter: softening what you say, and decoding what the other side says.

Opening and setting the scene

  • "Thanks for making the time. It'd be good to find something that works for both of us."
  • "Shall we start by going over what each of us is looking for?"
  • "From our side, the main priorities are price and delivery time."

Stating your priorities early is not giving too much away. It shapes the conversation around what matters to you.

Making and asking for offers

  • "What sort of figure did you have in mind?"
  • "We'd be looking at around €40 per unit."
  • "Our standard rate is X, but there's some flexibility depending on volume."
  • "Is that your best offer?"

Notice "we'd be looking at" and "around". They make your number sound like a starting point while still anchoring the conversation.

The key structure: conditional bargaining

Never give something without getting something back. In English, you say this with a conditional.

  • "If you can commit to 500 units, we could bring the price down to €38."
  • "We'd be prepared to extend the payment terms, provided that you sign for two years."
  • "As long as delivery is guaranteed by March, we can accept that price."
  • "What if we split the shipping costs?"

"Could", "would" and "prepared to" are doing important work. "We will reduce the price" sounds like a promise already made. "We could reduce the price if…" keeps it as an offer on the table, only valid if they give something too.

Saying no politely

Impossible.I don't think we can go that far, but we could look at…

The best refusals end with an alternative. A no followed by "but we could…" keeps the negotiation moving. A no on its own stops it.

Buying time

You never have to decide in the room. These phrases are normal and professional:

  • "I'll need to check that with my team."
  • "Let me take that away and come back to you by Thursday."
  • "Can we take a five-minute break?"
  • "I'm not in a position to agree to that today."

"I'm not in a position to…" is particularly useful. It says no for now without blaming anyone.

Clarifying and summarising

Numbers, dates and conditions need to be crystal clear. Misunderstandings here are expensive.

  • "Just to clarify, is that price per unit or per pallet?"
  • "Does that include VAT?"
  • "So, if I've understood correctly, you're offering…"
  • "Let's go over what we've agreed so far."

Closing the deal

  • "I think we have a deal."
  • "That sounds acceptable to us."
  • "Great. I'll put that in writing and send it over today."
  • "It's been good doing business with you."

Always confirm in writing straight away. A short email listing the agreed price, quantity, dates and conditions prevents most later disputes.

How to read British negotiation language

Some phrases mean more, or less, than they seem:

  • "That's an interesting proposal" may mean "We don't like it."
  • "We'll bear that in mind" often means "We won't do that."
  • "I hear what you're saying" means "I understand, and I still disagree."
  • "That might be a bit of a stretch" usually means "No."

Context and tone matter, so if you're unsure, ask: "Is that something you could consider, or is it off the table?" There's more on this in understanding British understatement at work.

A tip from lessons: prepare your numbers in English

The place I see confident speakers stumble is numbers. Under pressure, "€1.25 million" or "fifteen per cent" can come out wrong, or slowly. Before a negotiation, say your key figures out loud several times: your opening offer, your limit and your likely compromise. It sounds basic. It makes a real difference to how confident you sound when the moment comes. And if you need to refuse something, the guide on how to disagree politely gives you the structure.
