ECL C1 sample letter and essay, marked

Two model ECL C1 texts, a formal email and an essay, with comments against each of the five official criteria. Use them to see what "covering a bullet point" really looks like at C1.

First, what the official sample shows

ECL publishes an assessed C1 writing sample: two texts of about 205 words each, scored by an assessor on the five criteria. The first text scored 3 for grammar and 3 for written accuracy, but 5 for style and 5 for communicative effectiveness. The second scored 4, 4, 4, 5 and 5.

The lesson: a text with some grammar and punctuation mistakes can still score top marks for organisation and task completion. Those two criteria depend on planning, not on perfect English, and they're where I'd spend your effort first.

Source: ECL, assessed C1 writing sample, and Criteria for assessing writing. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

The two texts below are mine, written as models. I've commented on each criterion, but I haven't given them official-looking scores: only ECL's assessors award marks, and two assessors can see a text slightly differently.

Text 1: a formal email

Practice task (my own, in the ECL style): Your company is organising a two-day conference and you are responsible for the venue. Write an email (around 200 words) to a hotel. Include: the details of your event; the facilities you need; a question about cost; why you need an answer quickly.

Model answer (205 words, including greeting and sign-off):

Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing on behalf of Northgate Consulting to enquire about hosting our annual client conference at your hotel. The event will take place on 14 and 15 May and we expect around 120 delegates, roughly a third of whom will need overnight accommodation.

In terms of facilities, we would require a main conference room seating 120 in theatre style, as well as three smaller rooms for parallel workshops. Reliable wi-fi and a projector in each room are essential, and we would also appreciate a quiet area where speakers could prepare.

Could you send me a quotation for the full package, including lunch and refreshments on both days? It would be particularly helpful if you could indicate whether a reduced rate is available for bookings of this size, and what your cancellation terms are.

I would be grateful for a reply by the end of next week, as we need to confirm the venue before invitations are sent out at the beginning of March. If your hotel is unable to accommodate us on these dates, please let me know as soon as possible so that we can make alternative arrangements.

I look forward to hearing from you.

Yours faithfully,
Anna Kovács

Comments

  • Communicative effectiveness: each bullet point has its own paragraph, and each is developed with detail rather than mentioned in passing. The reason for urgency is a concrete one, the invitation date, not just "please reply soon".
  • Style: consistently formal. No contractions, polite indirect requests ("I would be grateful for…", "It would be particularly helpful if…"), and the right opening and closing for an unknown reader.
  • Vocabulary: precise, topic-specific language: "delegates", "theatre style", "parallel workshops", "quotation", "cancellation terms", "alternative arrangements".
  • Formal accuracy: a range of structures without strain: a relative clause ("a third of whom"), a conditional, a passive ("invitations are sent out").
  • Written accuracy: clear paragraphs, one purpose each, and consistent punctuation.

What a weaker version often does: combines the facilities and cost into one paragraph, and the fourth bullet point becomes one line, "Please answer quickly because it is urgent." Covered, but not developed, and development is what the top band asks for.

Text 2: an essay

Task (from ECL's official C1 writing sample): An American communication company calls for entries to an essay competition on the role of the internet. Write an essay (around 200 words) about: the opportunities and forms of virtual communication; the effects of the web on personal contacts; the reliability of the information gathered from the internet; internet and data protection.

Source: ECL C1 writing sample task. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

Model answer (200 words):

The internet has changed not only how we communicate, but who we communicate with. Its benefits are enormous, yet they come at a price.

Virtual communication now takes many forms, from instant messaging and video calls to online communities built around shared interests. For people separated by distance, a weekly video call can keep a relationship alive in a way letters never could.

However, the effect on personal contact is mixed. While we keep in touch with more people than ever, many of those connections are shallow, and time spent scrolling is often time taken away from the people in the same room.

A further problem is reliability. Anyone can publish anything, and false information often spreads faster than corrections. Checking the source and comparing several reports has become a basic skill, which arguably schools should teach explicitly.

Finally, there is the question of data protection. Every free service is paid for, in part, with personal information, and most users have little idea how it is stored or shared. Stronger regulation helps, but individuals also need to be more careful about what they share.

On balance, the internet is a remarkable tool, provided we use it critically rather than passively.

Comments

  • Communicative effectiveness: all four bullet points, in order, one paragraph each, each with an opinion and a reason or example.
  • Style: an essay voice, with a clear thesis in the introduction and a conclusion that answers it. Linking does real work: "However", "A further problem", "Finally", "On balance".
  • Vocabulary: "shallow" connections, "spreads faster than corrections", "use it critically rather than passively". Precise rather than showy.
  • Formal accuracy: varied sentence types, including contrast clauses ("While we keep in touch…") and a passive ("is paid for").
  • Written accuracy: six short, clearly separated paragraphs.

How to use these models

  1. Don't memorise them. Your task will have different bullet points.

  2. Copy the plan, not the text. One paragraph per bullet point, a short introduction, a one-line conclusion.

  3. Write the essay task yourself in 35 minutes, then compare paragraph by paragraph. Is each bullet point developed, or only mentioned?
