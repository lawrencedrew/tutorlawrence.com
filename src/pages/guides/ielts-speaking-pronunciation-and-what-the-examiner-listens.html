IELTS Speaking: pronunciation and what the examiner listens for

You don't need a British or American accent for a high Pronunciation score. The examiner listens for how easily they can understand you, and for stress, rhythm, chunking and intonation. Those can be practised.

Pronunciation is a quarter of your Speaking score

Speaking is marked on four criteria: Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, and Pronunciation. They carry equal weight. Many candidates spend all their time on vocabulary and ignore a quarter of the marks.

Source: IELTS, IELTS scoring in detail. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

What the criterion actually covers

The official description lists these features:

  • Chunking: dividing speech into meaningful groups of words.
  • Rhythm, stress timing and linking of sounds, including connected speech.
  • Stress and intonation used to add meaning, for example to contrast or emphasise.
  • Individual sounds: word stress, vowels and consonants.
  • Listener effort: how hard the examiner has to work to understand you.
  • The effect of accent on intelligibility.

Look at the last point carefully. The question isn't "does this person have an accent?" but "does the accent get in the way?" At band 8, the descriptors say accent has minimal effect on intelligibility. At band 9, none. Everyone has an accent, including the examiner.

Source: IELTS, Speaking key assessment criteria and Speaking band descriptors. Check them before your exam in case anything has changed.

The band 6 trap

The band 6 description is worth reading closely, because it's where many good speakers get stuck. It says chunking is generally appropriate, but rhythm may be affected by a lack of stress-timing and/or a rapid speech rate, and that stress and intonation are used effectively at times but not sustained.

"A rapid speech rate" surprises people. Students often think speaking fast sounds fluent. In fact, rushing tends to flatten the rhythm: every syllable gets the same weight, so nothing stands out, and the listener has to work harder. Slowing down a little and stressing the key words usually sounds more fluent, not less.

Four things to practise

1. Stress the words that carry the meaning

English is stress-timed. The important words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives) are long and clear; the small words (a, the, of, to, can) are short and weak. Say this sentence, stressing only the capitals:

"I WORK in a SMALL TEAM, so I DEAL with CLIENTS DIRECTLY."

If every word gets equal stress, it's correct but hard to follow. My guide to word stress covers the word-level side of this.

2. Pause in chunks, not randomly

Pauses are fine. Pauses in the wrong place aren't. Compare:

  • Hard to follow: "I think that the | main reason why people | move to | cities is jobs."
  • Easy to follow: "I think the main reason | people move to cities | is jobs."

Each chunk is a unit of meaning. Practise by marking chunks with a pencil in a short paragraph and reading it aloud.

3. Use stress to contrast

This is a band 7–8 feature that's easy to show in Part 3. When you compare two things, stress the difference:

"OLDER people tend to prefer shops, but YOUNGER people buy almost everything ONLINE."

"It's not that I DISLIKE it. I just don't have TIME for it."

Moving the stress changes the meaning, and the examiner hears that you can control it.

4. Link words together

Native speakers join a final consonant to a following vowel: "an_apple", "turn_it_off", "pick_it_up". Students often separate every word carefully, which sounds robotic. Practise a few common phrases until the linking is automatic. My guide to contractions and linking has more.

Common sound problems worth fixing

Only fix individual sounds that cause misunderstanding. A few I hear often from European speakers:

  • Short and long vowels: ship / sheep, full / fool, live / leave.
  • "th": "three" said as "tree" can confuse a number.
  • Final consonants dropped or devoiced: "bag" sounding like "back", or "worked" losing its -ed.
  • Word stress on the wrong syllable: deVELop, not DEvelop; PHOtograph but phoTOgraphy.

Word stress errors matter more than most sound errors. A listener can often guess a slightly wrong vowel, but a word stressed on the wrong syllable can become unrecognisable.

A ten-minute daily routine

  1. Pick a Part 3 question and answer it out loud for one minute. Record it.

  2. Listen back once, only for rhythm. Did the key words stand out?

  3. Write down your answer's two most important sentences, mark chunks and stress, and say them again.

  4. Record the whole answer again. Compare.

Don't do this with a memorised script. Answer a new question each day; the aim is to change how you speak, not to polish one answer.
