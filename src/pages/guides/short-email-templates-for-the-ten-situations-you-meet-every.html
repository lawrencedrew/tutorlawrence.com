Short email templates for the ten situations you meet every week

Most work emails fall into the same ten situations. Here's a short, natural template for each one. Change the words in square brackets and send.

How to use these templates

Each template is deliberately short. In English, a shorter email usually feels more professional, not less. Copy the ones you need into a notes file or your email app's saved replies. After a few weeks, you won't need to look at them, because the structure will be automatic.

Use "Hi [name]," and "Thanks," or "Best regards," around them. For formal versions, see opening and closing lines for English emails.

1. Asking for something

"Could you send me [the latest sales figures] by [Thursday]? I need them for [the monthly report]. Let me know if that's a problem."

Question, date, reason. If you only remember one template, make it this one.

2. Sending something

"I've attached [the updated contract]. The main changes are in [sections 3 and 5]. Let me know if you have any questions."

Tell the reader where to look. It saves them reading the whole document to find what's new.

3. Following up

"Just following up on my email below about [the training dates]. Could you let me know [which week works best] by [Friday]?"

Reply in the same thread so the original is underneath. More on this in how to follow up on an email without sounding rude.

4. Arranging a meeting

"Would you be free for a [30-minute] call next week to discuss [the Q2 budget]? I'm available [Tuesday after 2pm or Wednesday morning]. Let me know what suits you."

Always offer two or three specific options. "When are you free?" starts a long back-and-forth.

5. Rescheduling

"I'm sorry, but something has come up and I can't make [Thursday's meeting]. Could we move it to [Friday at the same time]? If that doesn't work, I'm also free [Monday morning]."

You don't need to explain exactly what came up. "Something has come up" is a normal, polite phrase that everyone accepts.

6. Saying no

"Thanks for thinking of me. Unfortunately, I can't [take this on] this week, as [I'm finishing the audit]. [I could help next week / Maria might be able to help]."

Thanks, no, reason, alternative. The full method is in how to say no politely in a business email.

7. Apologising for a mistake

"Sorry, I [sent the wrong version] earlier. [I've attached the correct one.] Thanks for your patience."

One sorry, the fix, and done. A long apology for a small mistake makes it look bigger.

8. Confirming what was agreed

"Thanks for your time today. Just to confirm what we agreed:

  • [You'll send the revised quote by Friday.]
  • [We'll confirm quantities by 15 March.]

Let me know if I've missed anything."

This one protects you. If there's a disagreement later, you have it in writing, and you've given the other person a chance to correct it.

9. Introducing two people

"[Anna], meet [Tom]. Tom runs [our logistics team] and is the best person to help with [your delivery questions]. Tom, Anna is [the project manager at Nordtech]. I'll leave you two to arrange a time."

Say who each person is and why they should talk. The last line politely removes you from the thread. The other person can then reply and move you to "Bcc".

10. Saying thank you

"Thanks for [your help with the presentation]. [The client was really pleased with the slides on pricing.] I really appreciate it."

The middle sentence is what makes it sincere. A specific detail shows you noticed what they did, not just that they did something.

Two words that make the templates sound natural

Look at how often "just" and "could" appear above. "Just following up", "just to confirm", "could you send". These small words soften a request without making it weak, and they're a big part of why British emails sound polite. Overuse them and you sound unsure, so use "just" once per email at most.

The other habit is contractions: "I've", "I'm", "can't", "we'll". Many students were taught that contractions are informal. In emails, they're normal at every level except the most formal letters. Without them, an email can sound stiff or even annoyed.

Adapting a template to your own job

Templates work best when you make them yours. Here's how I do it with students in lessons:

  1. Look at your sent folder from last week and count which of these ten situations came up.

  2. Pick the three most common.

  3. Rewrite those three templates with the real names, projects and phrases from your job.

  4. Save them where you can reach them quickly.

Ten minutes of work, and your most frequent emails are covered.
