How to run a meeting in English: phrases for each stage

Chairing a meeting in English is easier than taking part in one, because you control the structure. You need about a dozen phrases, one or two for each stage, and the confidence to use them.

Why chairing feels hard, and why it isn't

Many people tell me they're fine in meetings until they have to lead one. Suddenly they worry about every sentence. But the chair's language is the most predictable language in the whole meeting. You open, you set the agenda, you move between points, you bring people in, you summarise and you close. Each stage has stock phrases that native speakers use without thinking. Learn those, and the rest is just talking about your work.

1. Opening the meeting

  • "Shall we get started?"
  • "Right, let's make a start. Thanks for coming, everyone."
  • "I think everyone's here, so let's begin."
  • "We'll give it one more minute for people to join." (online)

"Right" or "OK, so" at the start is how British speakers signal "the meeting has begun". Say it a little louder than normal, then pause. People will stop chatting.

2. Stating the purpose and agenda

  • "The aim of today's meeting is to agree the launch date."
  • "We've got three things to cover. First…, then…, and finally…"
  • "We've got 30 minutes, so I'll keep us moving."
  • "Is there anything anyone wants to add to the agenda?"

Saying the end time out loud is one of the most useful things a chair can do. It gives you permission to move people on later without seeming rude.

3. Moving between points

  • "Let's start with the budget."
  • "OK, I think we've covered that. Let's move on to the timeline."
  • "That brings us to the next point."
  • "Let's come back to that at the end if we have time."

4. Bringing people in

A good chair makes sure the quieter people speak. Use names.

  • "Anna, what's your view on this?"
  • "Tomasz, you've worked with this supplier. Any thoughts?"
  • "We haven't heard from the sales side yet. Maria?"
  • "Does anyone see it differently?"

"Does anyone see it differently?" is far better than "Does everyone agree?" The second question invites silence. The first invites the honest objection you need to hear now, not after the meeting.

5. Keeping things on track

This is the stage people dread, because it means stopping someone. The trick is to thank them, then redirect.

  • "That's a good point, but I'm conscious of time. Can we take it offline?"
  • "Sorry to stop you there, Piotr. Could we pick that up separately?"
  • "I think we're getting a bit off track. Let's go back to the timeline."
  • "Let's park that for now." (put it aside to discuss later)

"I'm conscious of time" is a very British phrase, and very useful. It blames the clock, not the person.

6. Checking agreement and actions

  • "So, are we agreed that we go with option B?"
  • "Can I just check we're all on the same page?"
  • "Who's going to take that on?"
  • "Could you have that done by Friday?"

Every action needs three things: a person, a task and a date. If one is missing, ask for it before moving on: "And when could you have that by?"

7. Summarising and closing

  • "Let me just summarise what we've agreed."
  • "So, to recap: Anna will send the figures by Wednesday, and Marek will contact the supplier."
  • "Is there any other business?" (often shortened to "AOB")
  • "Great, I think that's everything. Thanks, everyone. I'll send round the notes."

Then follow up in writing. A short summary email the same day stops misunderstandings growing. There's a full guide on how to write a clear meeting summary email.

A sample meeting, start to finish

Chair: "Right, shall we get started? Thanks for joining. The aim today is to agree the new delivery schedule. We've got 20 minutes and two things to cover: the dates, then who tells the client."

Chair: "Let's start with the dates. Katarzyna, you've seen the warehouse plan. What's realistic?"

Katarzyna: "Probably the 14th, but it depends on the new forklift…"

Chair: "OK, let's park the forklift question for now. So, are we agreed on the 14th as a target? Good. Next point: the client."

Chair: "So, to recap: target date the 14th, and I'll call the client tomorrow. Any other business? No? Thanks, everyone."

That's a complete, well-run meeting, and nothing in it is difficult English.

What I tell students before their first time chairing

Write your opening two sentences and your closing summary line on paper before the meeting. Those are the moments when nerves are highest, and they're the parts everyone remembers. The middle takes care of itself once you've started well. If people talk over each other, the chair is the one person who's allowed to say "One at a time, please. Anna first, then Tomasz."
