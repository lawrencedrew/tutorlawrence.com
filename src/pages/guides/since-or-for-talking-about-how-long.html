'Since' or 'for'? Talking about how long

Use "since" with a starting point and "for" with a length of time. The harder part is the verb that goes with them, and that is where most mistakes happen.

The rule in one table

WordFollowed byExamples
sincea point in time: when it startedsince 2019, since March, since Monday, since 9 o'clock, since I joined the company
fora period of time: how longfor six years, for three months, for a week, for ages, for a long time

A simple check: if you can put "from" in front of it, it takes "since". If it answers the question "how long?", it takes "for". "From 2019" works, so since 2019. "From six years" doesn't, but "six years" answers "how long?", so for six years.

The tense is the real problem

In many European languages, you say the equivalent of "I work here since 2019" or "I work here for six years", in the present tense. English doesn't allow that. When the situation started in the past and is still true now, English uses the present perfect.

What people sayCorrect English
I work here since 2019.I've worked here since 2019.
I am in this role for three years.I've been in this role for three years.
We know each other since university.We've known each other since university.
How long do you work there?How long have you worked there?
I'm waiting for his reply since Monday.I've been waiting for his reply since Monday.

"I work here for six years" is particularly risky. A native speaker may hear it as a plan for the future: you intend to work here for six years.

Present perfect simple or continuous?

Both are correct with "since" and "for", and often the difference is small.

  • "I've worked here for six years" sounds like a fact, a stable situation.
  • "I've been working here for six years" puts more focus on the activity and its duration. It can sound slightly more personal, or slightly more tired.

Two practical points. First, verbs like know, be, have (meaning own) and believe don't normally go in the continuous: I've known her for years, not "I've been knowing her". Second, for something that feels temporary or annoying, the continuous is natural: I've been chasing this invoice for three weeks.

"For" with finished time

"For" can also describe a period that is completely over. Then you use the past simple.

  • I worked in Madrid for four years (I don't work there now).
  • I've worked in Madrid for four years (I still work there).

That one small difference matters in a job interview. Use the wrong one and the interviewer may think you've left a job you still have, or the opposite. For more on this, see when British speakers use the present perfect.

"Since" with a past event

"Since" is often followed by a whole clause, and that clause is in the past simple, because it describes the moment things started.

  • I've been much busier since I moved to the sales team.
  • Nothing has changed since we last spoke.
  • It's two years since we launched the product.

The last pattern, "It's [time] since…", is a very natural way to say how long ago something happened. It's been a while since we caught up is a friendly opening for an email to someone you haven't contacted recently.

Other words that get mixed up

"For" and "during"

"For" answers how long. "During" answers when, and it is followed by a noun, not a number. I was ill for three days during the conference. You can't say "during three days".

"For" and "ago"

"Ago" points back to a moment in the past and uses the past simple: I joined three years ago. Compare I've been here for three years. Both describe the same situation, from different angles.

Negatives: "for" or "in"

"I haven't seen him for months" is the traditional British form. "I haven't seen him in months" started as American English but is now very common in Britain too. Both are fine.

"Since" meaning "because"

"Since" can also mean "because", usually in more formal writing: Since the deadline has moved, we can review the draft again. This has nothing to do with time, so it takes whatever tense the meaning needs.

Answering "How long…?" in an interview

Here is a sample answer that uses all of this correctly:

"I've been a project manager for about five years. I started in logistics, where I worked for two years, and I've been at my current company since 2022. Since I joined, I've been leading the supplier side of the business."

Notice the switch: present perfect for what is still true, past simple ("worked", "started") for the finished job, past simple after "since" for the starting event ("joined"). If you can say a paragraph like this about your own career without hesitating, you have this topic under control. You can build it into your answer for "Tell me about yourself".
