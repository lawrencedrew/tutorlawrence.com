Phone and video calls: phrases for when the line is bad

When a call breaks up, the problem isn't your English, it's the connection. Say so clearly, early and with a fixed phrase, and the other person will repeat, slow down or call you back without any awkwardness.

Why people stay silent, and why they shouldn't

On a bad line, many people pretend they've heard. They nod, say "yes, yes", and hope the meaning becomes clear later. In a second language this is even more tempting, because you're not sure whether the problem is the audio or your listening.

Here's what I tell students: blame the line. Even if it's partly your listening, saying "you're breaking up" is completely acceptable, and it gets you a clear repetition. Native speakers do this all the time, including when the audio is fine and they simply weren't paying attention.

When the sound breaks up

  • "Sorry, you're breaking up a bit."
  • "You cut out for a second there. Could you say that again?"
  • "I missed the last part. Could you repeat it?"
  • "Sorry, the line's not great. I only caught half of that."

"Breaking up" and "cutting out" are the two key expressions. Both mean the sound keeps disappearing. "I didn't catch that" is the natural way to say you didn't hear something.

When they can't hear you

  • "Can you hear me OK?"
  • "Is my sound any better now?"
  • "Let me try moving closer to the router."
  • "I'll switch off my camera. That might help the connection."

When the video freezes

  • "You've frozen, I'm afraid."
  • "Your screen's frozen, but I can still hear you."
  • "I think I froze for a moment. What was the last thing you heard?"

"What was the last thing you heard?" is extremely useful. It saves you repeating everything and tells you exactly where to restart.

Echo, background noise and muting

  • "I'm getting a bit of an echo. Could everyone who isn't speaking mute, please?"
  • "Sorry, there's some background noise on someone's line."
  • "You're on mute, Tomasz." (the most common sentence on any video call)
  • "Sorry, I was on mute. What I was saying was…"

When the call drops

  • "Sorry, we got cut off."
  • "I think the call dropped. Shall I call you back?"
  • "Let's hang up and I'll ring you straight back."
  • "The connection's really bad. Could we switch to a phone call instead?"

Agree who calls back. If you both call at the same time, you'll both get a busy signal. "I'll call you" settles it.

Confirming important details

Bad lines make numbers, names and dates dangerous. Always repeat them back.

  • "Let me just read that back to you: order number 4-1-2, 9-8-6. Is that right?"
  • "Was that fifteen or fifty?"
  • "Could you spell your surname for me?"
  • "So that's Tuesday the 14th, at 3 o'clock UK time?"

Fifteen and fifty, thirteen and thirty: these pairs cause more confusion on calls than any vocabulary problem. Ask "one-five or five-zero?" if you're unsure. For spelling, say "B for Bravo" or "B as in Bristol". On a bad line, letters like B, D, P, T and V all sound similar.

A short example

A: "…so the shipment should arrive on the—"

B: "Sorry, Marco, you cut out there. When did you say it arrives?"

A: "The 18th. One-eight."

B: "The 18th, great. And can you hear me OK?"

A: "You're a bit quiet, but it's fine."

B: "OK, I'll send you an email with the date so we've both got it in writing."

Follow up in writing

After any call where the line was bad, send a two-line email confirming the key points: "Thanks for the call. Just to confirm, the shipment arrives on 18 March and you'll send the invoice by Friday." It protects both of you, and it's normal professional practice, not a sign of distrust. The guide on writing a clear meeting summary email shows how to keep these short.

A tip from lessons: say the problem first

The best moment to mention a bad connection is at the start, before you've missed anything important. "Just so you know, my connection's a bit unstable today. If I freeze, I'll call you back." It sounds organised, and it gives you permission to ask for repeats later without apologising every time.
