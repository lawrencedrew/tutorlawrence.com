A four-week IELTS study plan for working professionals

Four weeks won't transform your English, but it is enough to stop losing marks to timing, format and habits. This plan assumes about 45–60 minutes on weekdays and one longer session at the weekend.

Before you start: be honest about what four weeks can do

Real band improvements usually take months, not weeks. I explain why in how long it takes to raise your score by one band. What a month can do is make sure the examiner sees the English you already have. For most working professionals, that's worth half a band or more in at least one skill, because they've never practised under exam conditions.

So the plan has one rule: everything is timed. Reading 60 minutes, Writing 60 minutes, Listening about 30 minutes, Speaking 11–14 minutes. If you only practise without a clock, you're preparing for a different test.

Source: IELTS, test format in detail. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

Writing gets two days because Task 2 counts twice as much as Task 1, and writing is the skill busy people practise least. If your target is a minimum in each skill, move your extra day to whichever skill is lowest.

Week 1: find out where you stand

  • Weekend: a full, timed practice test in one sitting. It's tiring, which is the point. Score Listening and Reading yourself, and keep your essays for later.
  • Weekdays: learn the format of every question type. Most people know the overall structure but have never done a flow-chart completion or a matching headings task under time pressure.
  • Output: four scores, and a list of the question types where you lost the most marks.

Week 2: fix technique

  • Writing: learn one essay structure and use it every time. A structure that works for any Task 2 question is a good place to start. Plan for five minutes before writing a word.
  • Reading: practise moving on. Set a limit of about 90 seconds per question, and if you're stuck, guess, mark it and come back.
  • Listening: before each part, read the questions and predict the type of answer: a number, a name, a noun.
  • Weekend: one Reading paper and one Writing paper, both timed.

Week 3: fix your English where it costs most

Look at your corrected essays and speaking recordings and find the two or three errors you make again and again. Students often repeat the same few: articles, a favourite linking word used five times, or one tense used for everything. Fix those, not everything.

  • Speaking: stop preparing scripts. Answer new questions out loud every day with a 20-second pause to think, then listen back. Examiners can tell when an answer is memorised.
  • Writing: rewrite one old essay each week using your correction list. Rewriting teaches more than writing a new one.
  • Weekend: a second full practice test. Compare with Week 1, skill by skill.

Week 4: rehearse and rest

  • Monday to Wednesday: one more timed paper in your weakest skill, and a final speaking practice with a real person if you can.
  • Thursday and Friday: light work only. Re-read your correction list and your essay structure.
  • The day before: no new material. Check the route, your ID and your start time. Sleep.

Making it fit a working week

A few things I've seen work for people with demanding jobs:

  • Book your study like a meeting. If it's in the calendar at 7:30 am, it happens. If it's "this evening", it often doesn't.
  • Use your work. If you write reports in English, you already practise Task 1 skills: describing what changed, comparing, picking out what matters.
  • Protect the weekend session. If you miss a weekday, don't try to catch up. Missing the full practice test is what hurts.
  • Talk out loud. Answering a Part 3 question in the car on the way home is speaking practice. Silent reading about speaking is not.

What to do if you only have two weeks

Combine Weeks 1 and 2 into one week of format and technique, then go straight to Week 4. Skip Week 3. You won't fix grammar in a fortnight, but you can stop losing marks to timing and unfamiliar question types.
