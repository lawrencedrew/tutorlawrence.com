Interview phrases for buying time to think

A short pause before a good answer sounds thoughtful. Silence followed by "ehh…" sounds lost. The difference is a few ready-made phrases. Here are the ones native speakers actually use, and when to use each.

Why you need them more in a second language

In your own language, you think and speak at the same time. In English, many people need a moment to find the idea and another moment to find the words. That's normal. The problem is that the silence feels much longer to you than it does to the interviewer, so you panic and start talking before you're ready.

A good thinking phrase fills two or three seconds naturally and tells the interviewer, "I'm considering this properly." That's a positive signal, not a weakness.

For a hard or unexpected question

  • "That's a good question. Let me think about that for a second."
  • "Hmm, I haven't been asked that before. Let me think."
  • "Let me think of a good example."
  • "There are a couple of ways I could answer that."

A warning about "That's a good question": it works once. Say it before every answer and it becomes a nervous habit the interviewer starts counting. Rotate between two or three phrases.

For a question with several parts

  • "So, I'd say there are two main things here."
  • "Let me take the first part first."
  • "I'll start with…, and then come back to…"

These do two jobs. They buy time, and they give your answer a structure before you've even started, which makes it easier to follow.

Checking the question

Repeating the question in your own words is the most useful technique of all. It buys time, it confirms you've understood, and it often gets the interviewer to add helpful detail.

  • "So you're asking how I'd handle it if the deadline moved?"
  • "Just to make sure I understand, do you mean with clients or within the team?"
  • "When you say 'challenging', do you mean technically or with people?"

If you genuinely didn't catch the question, say so directly. See how to ask someone to repeat or slow down, naturally.

Small fillers that sound natural

Native speakers fill tiny gaps with short words. Using these instead of a sound from your own language makes you sound more fluent straight away:

FillerHow it's used
Well…At the start of an answer that needs some thought.
So…To begin an explanation or summary.
I mean…To clarify what you just said.
Let me see…When looking for a detail, like a date or a number.
How can I put it…When you're looking for the right word.

One thing I notice in lessons: people often keep the hesitation sound from their first language, like "eee", "ehm" or "pues". It's one of the strongest signals of a non-native speaker, more than grammar mistakes. Replacing it with "um" or "well" takes a couple of weeks of conscious effort and makes a surprisingly big difference.

When you can't think of an example

Competency questions ("Tell me about a time when…") are where people freeze most. Be honest and offer something close:

  • "I can't think of exactly that situation, but something similar happened when…"
  • "I haven't faced that directly, but here's how I'd approach it."
  • "Could I come back to that one? I'd like to give you a good example."

The last phrase is fine to use once in an interview. Make sure you do come back to it, though. Interviewers remember.

The real fix for freezing is preparation: five strong stories ready in advance. See the STAR method for interview answers.

When an answer goes wrong halfway through

You start a sentence, lose the grammar, and don't know how to finish. Don't fight it. Restart cleanly:

  • "Sorry, let me put that another way."
  • "Actually, let me start that again."
  • "What I mean is…"

Native speakers do this all the time. A clean restart sounds confident. Battling through a broken sentence for twenty seconds does not.

Phrases that don't work so well

AvoidWhy, and what to say
Wait, wait.Sounds like you're stopping the interviewer. Say "Let me think for a second."
Good question.Fine once. Every time sounds automatic.
I don't know.Ends the conversation. Say "I'm not sure, but my first thought would be…"
How to say…Should be "How can I put it…" or "What's the word…"

Practise them out loud

Choose three phrases you like, one from each section, and use them on purpose in your next few meetings or calls. Phrases you've only read won't come out under pressure. Phrases you've said ten times will. There's more on this in from knowing a word to using it.
