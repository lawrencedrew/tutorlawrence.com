Questions to ask at the end of an interview

When the interviewer says "Do you have any questions for us?", the answer is always yes. Prepare three or four, ask two, and use them to show you've been listening. Here are the ones that work, and how to phrase them.

Why "No, I think you've covered everything" is a missed chance

It's polite, and sometimes true, but it ends the interview flatly. Your questions are the last thing the interviewer hears from you. They show interest, they show how you think, and they give you information you genuinely need to decide whether you want the job.

Questions about the role

  • "What would a typical week look like in this role?"
  • "What would you expect me to achieve in the first three months?"
  • "What's the biggest challenge for whoever takes this job?"
  • "How will success in this role be measured?"

The second and third are my favourites. Their answers tell you exactly what the employer is worried about, and you can respond with a short example of how you've handled something similar.

Questions about the team

  • "Could you tell me a bit about the team I'd be working with?"
  • "Who would I work with most closely, outside the team?"
  • "How does the team usually communicate? Mostly meetings, or more in writing?"

That last one is especially useful if you'll be working in English for the first time. A team that works mostly in written chat feels very different from one with daily calls.

Questions about the company and growth

  • "What are the team's priorities for the next year?"
  • "How do people usually develop from this role?"
  • "What do you enjoy most about working here?"

The last question is personal and slightly informal, which is exactly why it works well with British interviewers. It turns the end of the interview into a conversation.

Questions about next steps

  • "What are the next steps in the process?"
  • "When are you hoping to make a decision?"
  • "Is there anything else I can send you that would help?"

Always ask one of these. It's practical, and it means you won't spend two weeks wondering when to follow up. When the time comes, see how to follow up on an email without sounding rude.

The best question is one you didn't prepare

Here's the tip I give everyone. During the interview, make a quick note of one thing the interviewer mentions: a new system, a reorganisation, a big client. Then ask about it at the end:

"You mentioned earlier that you're moving to a new CRM next year. How is the team finding that change?"

"You mentioned earlier that…" is the most powerful phrase in this whole section. It proves you were listening, and no other candidate can ask that exact question.

Questions to avoid

AvoidWhy, and what to ask instead
What does your company do?You should know this already. Ask about a specific product or project instead.
How many holidays do I get?Too early in a first interview. Ask at the offer stage.
Can I work from home every day?Ask more openly: "How does the team usually split office and remote work?"
Did I get the job?Puts the interviewer on the spot. Ask "What are the next steps?"

Salary is a special case. If the interviewer hasn't mentioned it and you're in a first round, it's usually better to wait. There's more on timing and phrasing in how to talk about your salary expectations in English.

How to phrase questions so they sound natural

Direct questions like "What is the biggest challenge?" are fine in English, but adding a soft opening makes them sound warmer:

  • "I'd be interested to know…"
  • "Could I ask about…?"
  • "I was wondering…"
  • "Just to follow up on something you said earlier…"

Watch the word order after these openings. It's an indirect question, so the verb doesn't move:

AvoidSay instead
I was wondering what is the biggest challenge.I was wondering what the biggest challenge is.
Could you tell me how big is the team?Could you tell me how big the team is?
I'd like to know when will you decide.I'd like to know when you'll decide.

It's one of the most common mistakes I hear from advanced speakers, and it's easy to fix once you notice it.

How to close

After your questions, finish with one short sentence: "Thank you, that's really helpful. I've enjoyed hearing more about the role, and I'm even more interested now." Then stop. No long speech needed.
