From knowing a word to using it: active vocabulary

You recognise thousands of words you never say. That gap closes when you stop learning single words and start practising the whole phrase you would actually use, out loud, within a few days.

Passive and active: what the difference really is

Your passive vocabulary is every word you understand when you read or hear it. Your active vocabulary is the much smaller set you reach for without thinking when you speak or write. For almost every learner the passive set is far bigger, and that is normal. Native speakers have the same gap.

The problem is when the gap stays wide at work. I see this every week: a student understands a report full of words like "mitigate", "allocate" and "feasible", then in the meeting about that report says "we need to make the risk smaller" and "is it possible?". Nothing is wrong with that English. It just doesn't show what they know, and it makes them feel less capable than they are.

Why reading more doesn't fix it

Recognising a word and producing it are different skills. To understand "mitigate" you only need a rough meaning. To use it, you need much more:

  • What goes with it. You mitigate a risk or an impact. You don't mitigate a person or a problem in most contexts.
  • The grammar around it. "Mitigate the risk", not "mitigate against the risk" in careful writing (though you will hear the second).
  • The register. "Mitigate" suits a report or a formal meeting. With a colleague over coffee, "reduce the risk" sounds more natural.
  • The sound. If you are not sure how to say it, you will avoid it. Most people don't notice they do this.

That last point is the one a working teacher sees most clearly. When a student avoids a word they obviously know, the reason is very often pronunciation, not meaning. Check the stress and the sounds before you try to use a new word, and it becomes much easier to say. My guide to word stress covers this.

Learn the chunk, not the word

Instead of writing "mitigate = reduce" in a notebook, write the phrase you would actually say, from your own job:

Single word in a listChunk you can use tomorrow
allocateWe've allocated two people to the project.
feasibleIs that feasible by the end of March?
outlineLet me briefly outline the plan.
bottleneckThe bottleneck is the approval stage.
flagI just wanted to flag a possible issue.

A chunk carries its collocations, grammar and register with it. When you need it in a meeting, it comes out whole, so you are not building a sentence around a lonely word under pressure.

A five-step routine for each new word

  1. Choose carefully. Pick words you will genuinely need in the next month. Five useful words a week beats fifty you will never say.

  2. Find it in context. Copy the sentence where you met it, then look at two or three more real examples so you can see what usually comes before and after it.

  3. Check the sound. Listen to it in a dictionary, notice the stressed syllable, and say it ten times out loud. Yes, out loud.

  4. Write your own sentence. Make it true and about your work. "I need to flag that the supplier is late again" will stay with you far longer than an invented example.

  5. Use it on purpose within 72 hours. Put it in an email, a message or a meeting. The first real use is the step that turns it from passive to active.

Spaced review that takes ten minutes

Once a word is in your notebook, come back to it a day later, then a week later, then a month later. Cover the English and try to produce the chunk from a prompt in your own language or a short note to yourself, for example "tell the team about a risk, softly". If you can say "I just wanted to flag a possible issue" without looking, it is active. If you can't, it goes back in the pile.

Flashcard apps are fine for this, as long as the card asks you to produce English, not just recognise it. Recognition cards only grow the passive side.

Upgrade words you already use

The quickest wins don't come from rare words. They come from replacing the safe, general words you overuse with more precise ones you already half-know. Students often lean on "do", "make", "get", "big" and "problem". Try this: record yourself describing your week for two minutes, listen back, and underline every general word. Then choose one better option for each:

  • "We have a big problem with the supplier" becomes "We have a serious delay with the supplier".
  • "I did the report" becomes "I finished the report" or "I put together the report".
  • "We got good results" becomes "We achieved strong results" in writing, or "the results were really good" in speech.

Precise verbs make a bigger difference than fancy adjectives. If make and do are one of your habits, see make or do: the rule and the exceptions.

Accept the awkward first attempts

The first few times you use a new word, it will feel strange in your mouth, and you may get the collocation slightly wrong. That is part of the process, not a sign you should go back to the safe word. Native listeners rarely notice a slightly unusual phrase. They do notice when someone with a good brain speaks in very simple words all the time.

One practical tip: tell yourself you will use one target chunk per meeting. Just one. It is small enough that you will actually do it, and over a month that is twenty or more new phrases in real use.
