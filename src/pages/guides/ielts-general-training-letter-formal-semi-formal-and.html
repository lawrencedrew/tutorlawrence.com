IELTS General Training letter: formal, semi-formal and informal

The right tone depends on one thing: who you're writing to and how well you know them. Get that decision right in the first minute and the rest of the letter becomes much easier to write.

What the task asks

In General Training Writing Task 1 you write a letter of at least 150 words about an everyday situation, such as a problem with your accommodation. The question gives you three bullet points to cover, and you should spend no more than 20 minutes on it. The letter may be personal, semi-formal or formal, depending on who you are writing to and how well you know them.

The sample tasks also tell you that you don't need to write any addresses, and they often give you the opening line to use, such as "Dear Sir or Madam,". If you're given an opening, that's a strong clue about the tone.

Sources: General Training test format in detail and the General Training Writing sample tasks (PDF), both on ielts.org. Check them before your exam in case anything has changed.

Why tone is marked

Tone is part of Task Achievement. In the official band descriptors for General Training letters, band 7 includes "It presents a clear purpose. The tone is consistent and appropriate to the task.", while band 5 says "the tone may be variable and sometimes inappropriate". So a letter to a friend that suddenly says "I am writing to inform you" costs marks, even if the grammar is perfect.

How to decide in ten seconds

You are writing to…StyleOpen withClose with
A company or official you don't know by nameFormalDear Sir or Madam,Yours faithfully,
A named person you don't know well, such as a manager, landlord or neighbourSemi-formalDear Mr Patel, / Dear Ms Novak,Yours sincerely, or Kind regards,
A friend or relativeInformalDear Anna, / Hi Tom,Best wishes, / Love, / See you soon,

A useful test: would you use their first name if you met them? If yes, write informally. If you'd use their surname, write semi-formally. If you don't know who they are, write formally.

"Ms" is the safe choice for a woman when you don't know whether she's married. And a small British point: we don't put a full stop after Mr, Mrs or Ms.

The same message in three styles

Formal, to a hotel: "I am writing to complain about the condition of the room I stayed in on 14 March. I would be grateful if you could arrange a partial refund."

Semi-formal, to your landlord: "I'm writing about the heating in the flat, which has stopped working again. Would it be possible for someone to look at it this week?"

Informal, to a friend: "Sorry it's taken me so long to write back. You won't believe what happened with my flat last week."

Notice the differences. Formal letters avoid contractions and use indirect requests ("I would be grateful if…"). Semi-formal letters can use contractions and direct but polite questions. Informal letters sound like you're talking, with phrasal verbs and a friendly opening.

Useful lines for each style

Formal

  • "I am writing to enquire about…"
  • "I would appreciate it if you could…"
  • "I look forward to hearing from you."

Semi-formal

  • "I'm writing to let you know that…"
  • "Could you let me know whether…?"
  • "Thanks for your help with this."

Informal

  • "It was great to hear from you."
  • "Guess what? I've finally…"
  • "Let me know what you think. Can't wait to see you."

Covering the three bullet points

Each bullet point needs real content. At band 7 all bullet points are covered and clearly highlighted. At band 5, one or more may not be covered properly. The easiest way to make sure you cover them is one paragraph per bullet point, in the order they're given, between a short opening and a short closing.

A typical plan for 160–190 words:

  1. Opening line: why you're writing.

  2. Bullet 1: two or three sentences.

  3. Bullet 2: two or three sentences, with a specific detail.

  4. Bullet 3: two or three sentences, usually a request or suggestion.

  5. Closing line and sign-off.

Invent specific details. "The shower has been leaking since Monday and the bathroom floor is now damaged" gives the examiner far more evidence of your English than "There are some problems with the bathroom".

Mistakes I see every week

  • Mixing styles. "Hi John, I am writing to inform you that I shall be unable to attend" is friendly at the start and stiff in the middle. Pick one style and keep it.
  • "Yours faithfully" after a name. In British English, "Yours faithfully" goes with "Dear Sir or Madam". Use "Yours sincerely" after a name.
  • Over-formal informal letters. Writing to a friend with "Furthermore" and "Nevertheless" sounds strange to a native reader.
  • Forgetting the purpose. By the end of your first paragraph, the reader should know exactly why you're writing.
