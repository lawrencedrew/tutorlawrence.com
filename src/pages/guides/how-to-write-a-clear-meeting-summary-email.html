How to write a clear meeting summary email

A good meeting summary isn't a record of everything that was said. It lists what was decided, who will do what, and by when, in a format people can read in thirty seconds.

Why it's worth sending

People leave meetings with different memories of what was agreed. A short summary, sent the same day, fixes the agreement in writing. It also makes you look organised, and in a second language, it gives you a chance to check you understood everything correctly. If something in the meeting went too fast, the summary is where you find out.

The structure

  1. Subject line with the meeting name and date: "Summary: supplier review, 12 March".

  2. One line of thanks and purpose. "Thanks for your time today. Here's a quick summary of what we agreed."

  3. Decisions. What was agreed, as short bullet points.

  4. Actions. Each action with an owner and a deadline.

  5. Open questions (if any). Things still to be decided.

  6. Next meeting and a request to correct anything you've missed.

The order matters. Decisions and actions go near the top, because some readers will stop after the first screen.

Write actions so nobody can misunderstand them

VagueClear
Look into the shipping costs.Pablo to compare quotes from three carriers by Friday 15 March.
We need to talk to finance.Hanna to ask finance about the budget increase before the next meeting.
Update the presentation.Me: add the Q1 figures to the client presentation by Wednesday.
Somebody should contact the client.Tom to call the client on Monday to confirm the new dates.

Every clear action has three parts: who, what (with a strong verb), and when. "Name to verb by date" is the standard pattern, and it reads naturally in English even though it isn't a full sentence.

Useful phrases

Opening

  • Thanks for your time this morning. Here's a quick summary.
  • Following today's meeting, here are the main points and actions.
  • Just to recap what we agreed…

Decisions

  • We agreed to…
  • We decided not to go ahead with…
  • We'll go with option B.

Open points

  • Still to be decided: …
  • We'll come back to the pricing question next week.
  • Pending confirmation from the client: …

Closing

  • Let me know if I've missed anything or got anything wrong.
  • Please reply by Thursday if you have any changes.
  • Our next meeting is on Tuesday 19 March at 10am.

A full example

"Subject: Summary: website relaunch meeting, 12 March

Hi all,

Thanks for your time today. Here's a quick summary.

Decisions

  • The new site will launch on 2 June, not 15 May.
  • We'll keep the current blog and move it across later.

Actions

  • Lea to send the final page list to the agency by Friday 15 March.
  • Marek to confirm the hosting budget with finance by 20 March.
  • Me: book user testing for the week of 13 May.

Still to decide

  • Whether we need a Polish version at launch. Marek will check with the sales team.

Next meeting: Tuesday 26 March, 10am. Let me know if I've missed anything.

Thanks,
Joanna"

Common mistakes

  • Writing minutes instead of a summary. "Then Marek said… and Lea answered…" is a story. Readers want outcomes.
  • Using "we" for actions. "We will check the budget" means nobody will. Name a person.
  • Sending it days later. Send it the same day while everyone remembers. A short summary today is better than a perfect one next week.
  • Tense confusion. Decisions are past ("We agreed") or future ("We'll launch"). Actions use "to + verb": "Lea to send". Avoid "Lea will be sending", which sounds less certain.

A trick for when you didn't catch everything

If the meeting moved fast and you're not sure you understood a point, don't guess. Write it as a question in the summary: "Just to check: did we agree the budget is €40,000 including VAT, or excluding?" This is completely normal in English, and native speakers do it too.
