ECL C1 reading: strategies for the gap-fill and matching tasks

ECL C1 reading gives you 45 minutes for two texts and 20 items, and every item is worth the same. Most lost points come from method, not vocabulary: reading too much too slowly, and trusting a word that merely appears in both places.

What you are up against

  • 45 minutes, two tasks, 1,000–1,300 words of text in total.
  • 20 items, each worth 1.25 points, so 25 points in total.
  • Text types at C1 include reports, blog posts, comments, brochures and newspaper articles.
  • Task types across ECL reading are multiple choice with three options, matching short texts to titles, statements or questions, gap-filling and short answers.
  • No dictionary, and no partial credit. An answer with anything extra or wrong in it scores zero.

Since August 2026 you need at least 60% in reading to pass the module, which is 12 correct items out of 20.

Source: ECL exam format page and Passing ECL exams. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

Timing: about 20 minutes per text

Give yourself 20 minutes for each task and keep 5 minutes at the end to transfer and check answers. Only answers on the answer sheet are marked, and they must be in black or blue ink. You can't use correction fluid or erasable pens: cross out a mistake and write the new answer clearly.

If one item takes more than two minutes, mark it, move on and come back. Twenty items at 1.25 points each means no single item deserves five minutes.

Gap-fill: read for the grammar of the gap

ECL's format page lists gap-filling without fixing exactly what goes in the gaps, so practise with both single words and longer chunks such as phrases or sentences. The method is the same:

  1. Read the whole text quickly first, for about two minutes. You need the line of argument before you fill anything.

  2. Look at both sides of each gap. What comes before tells you the grammar. What comes after tells you the logic. Students often read only the sentence before the gap.

  3. Predict before you look at the options. Say to yourself "this needs a contrast" or "this needs an example". Then find the option that does that job.

  4. Check the reference words. "This", "these", "such", "it" and "the latter" must point to something that makes sense. If an option starts "These measures…", there must be measures just before the gap.

  5. Reread the paragraph with your answer in place. If it sounds odd out loud in your head, it probably is.

An example of reference-word logic:

"Several cities have introduced congestion charges. ______ Critics, however, argue that the charges hit low-income drivers hardest."

  • A. Supporters point to cleaner air and faster buses as a result.
  • B. This is why most people prefer public transport.

The answer is A. "Critics, however" needs a contrasting group before it, and "supporters" provides one. B looks plausible, but nothing in the text so far supports "most people prefer".

Matching: beware the shared word

In matching tasks you connect short texts or paragraphs with titles, statements or questions. The trap is always the same: a statement repeats a word from the wrong paragraph, while the right paragraph says the same thing in other words.

Statement: "The writer regrets a decision made for financial reasons."

  • Paragraph 2 mentions "money" and "budget" three times, but is about planning a holiday.
  • Paragraph 4 says: "Looking back, I wish I hadn't turned down the job just to save on rent."

Paragraph 4 is the match. "I wish I hadn't" is the regret, "to save on rent" is the financial reason. No shared vocabulary at all.

What works:

  • Underline the key idea in each statement, not the key word. "Regrets" plus "financial reason" is the idea.
  • Match the easiest ones first to reduce the options for the hard ones.
  • Check whether options can be used twice. The instructions tell you. Don't assume.
  • Watch for partial matches. A paragraph that matches half a statement is a distractor. The right one matches all of it.

Short answers: fewer words, more points

The exam regulations are clear that short questions need a few words, not full sentences, and that an answer with unnecessary information is marked wrong, including when it's copied straight from the text.

Loses the pointGets the point
"Because the company had expanded too quickly and also the market changed in 2019""It expanded too quickly"
"In the second half of the article it says the survey covered 2,000 people""2,000 people"

Only add more if the question asks for two things. If it asks for one reason, give one.

Multiple choice: eliminate, don't select

With three options, find the two wrong answers rather than the right one. A wrong option usually does one of these things:

  • says something true that the text doesn't connect to the question
  • goes further than the text: "always", "all", "proves"
  • reverses the cause and the effect

How to practise in the weeks before

  • Use ECL's own sample test booklets, which include complete tests with answer keys. Keep real ECL material for timed practice.
  • Between tests, read one opinion article a day from a quality English newspaper. After each paragraph, write a four-word summary. That habit is exactly what matching needs.
  • Keep an error log with one line per wrong answer: which task type, and why. After three tests, the pattern is usually obvious.
