How IELTS Writing is marked: the four criteria in plain English

Your writing is judged on four things, each worth a quarter of the score. Once you know what each one actually rewards, you can stop guessing and work on the one that is holding you back.

The basics

The Writing test has two tasks in 60 minutes. Each task gets its own band from 0 to 9, based on four equally weighted criteria, 25% each. Task 2 counts twice as much as Task 1 towards your Writing score.

CriterionThe question it asks
Task Achievement (Task 1) / Task Response (Task 2)Did you do what the question asked, fully and relevantly?
Coherence and CohesionIs it easy to follow, and are ideas connected well?
Lexical ResourceHow wide and how accurate is your vocabulary?
Grammatical Range and AccuracyHow varied and how accurate is your grammar?

Source: the British Council's IELTS Writing test format page. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

1. Task Achievement / Task Response

This is about content, not language. In Task 2, the band 7 descriptor asks for "a clear and developed position", with the main parts of the prompt "appropriately addressed" and main ideas "extended and supported". At band 6, the conclusions "may be unclear, unjustified or repetitive", and some ideas "may be insufficiently developed".

In plain English: answer every part of the question, make your opinion obvious, and explain each idea properly instead of listing several.

In Task 1 Academic the key word is overview. Band 7 asks for "a clear overview" with main trends or differences identified. In General Training letters, it's covering all three bullet points with a clear purpose and a consistent tone.

Check yourself: could someone read only your introduction and conclusion and know exactly what you think?

2. Coherence and Cohesion

Coherence is whether your ideas are in a logical order. Cohesion is how you connect them: linking words, but also pronouns like "this" and "they", and words that refer back, such as "this problem" or "such measures".

The band 7 descriptor mentions "a range of cohesive devices including reference and substitution" used flexibly, and paragraphing that is "generally used effectively". At band 6, cohesion "may be faulty or mechanical".

In plain English: one main idea per paragraph, and connections that sound natural rather than ticked off a list. "Mechanical" is the word I quote most often to students. It describes the essay where every sentence starts with "Moreover" or "Furthermore".

Check yourself: cover the first word of each sentence. Does the paragraph still flow? If it does, some of those linkers can probably go.

3. Lexical Resource

Vocabulary range and accuracy. At band 7, there is "some ability to use less common and/or idiomatic items" and the vocabulary allows "some flexibility and precision". At band 6, the vocabulary is "generally adequate and appropriate", with meaning clear "in spite of a rather restricted range or a lack of precision in word choice".

In plain English: precise words beat impressive words. "Commuting costs" is better than "expenses related to transportation activities". Natural word partnerships matter: we say "a heavy workload", "make a decision", "a serious problem".

A common trap is forcing rare words in. A rare word used wrongly costs you more than a simple word used well.

Check yourself: did you repeat the same key noun five times? Could some of those be more precise?

4. Grammatical Range and Accuracy

At band 7, "a variety of complex structures is used with some flexibility and accuracy" and "error-free sentences are frequent". At band 6, there is "a mix of simple and complex sentence forms", but complex structures aren't as accurate as simple ones.

In plain English: you need both range and accuracy. Long sentences full of mistakes don't help. Short sentences with no mistakes don't show enough range. Aim for a mix: relative clauses, conditionals, passives where they fit, and plenty of clean, correct sentences.

Punctuation is included here. Run-on sentences joined with commas are a very common problem.

Check yourself: count how many of your sentences contain no errors at all. That number matters more than how complex the best one is.

Quotations above are from the IELTS Writing band descriptors (PDF) on ielts.org. Check them before your exam in case anything has changed.

Things that pull your score down

  • Being under length. The minimum is 150 words in Task 1 and 250 in Task 2, and the band descriptors take an under-length answer into account, so a short answer can lose marks.
  • Copying the question. The band descriptors say copied wording from the prompt is discounted.
  • Memorised phrases. The lower band descriptors mention over-dependence on memorised language. Ready-made openings rarely help.

How to use this

Most students have one criterion that sits half a band below the others. It's rarely the one they think. People worry about vocabulary when the real problem is an unclear position, or they work on linking words when the problem is grammar accuracy.

  1. Get one essay marked criterion by criterion, not just with a total.

  2. Pick the lowest criterion and work only on that for two weeks.

  3. Write, get marked, repeat. Checking against the descriptors each time.
