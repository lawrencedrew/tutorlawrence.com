IELTS Listening: how to avoid losing easy marks

Many Listening marks aren't lost because you didn't understand. They're lost to spelling, word limits, a speaker who changes their mind, or a moment of panic. All of those can be fixed.

How the test works

  • About 30 minutes, in four parts with 10 questions each, 40 questions in total.
  • Parts 1 and 2 are everyday social situations. Parts 3 and 4 are set in education and training.
  • You hear each recording once only.
  • A range of accents is used, including British, Australian, New Zealand and North American.
  • On paper, you get 10 minutes at the end to transfer your answers to the answer sheet. On computer, you get 2 minutes to check your answers.
  • One mark per correct answer, and no negative marking.

Sources: Academic test format in detail on ielts.org, and the British Council's Listening test format page and paper vs computer guide. Check them before your exam in case anything has changed.

1. Spelling counts

The British Council is clear that incorrect spelling is marked wrong. You can hear the answer perfectly and still lose the mark.

The words that cause most trouble are everyday ones that come up in Part 1 forms: accommodation, Wednesday, February, necessary, library, restaurant, business, environment, government. Make a list of the ones you get wrong in practice tests and learn them properly.

Also practise:

  • Spelled-out names and addresses. Know how English speakers say letters, especially A, E, I, G, J, R, W and Y. "Double T" means TT.
  • Numbers. Fifteen versus fifty, thirteen versus thirty. Listen to the stress: fifteen, fifty.
  • Phone numbers. British speakers often say "oh" for zero and "double five" for 55.

2. Respect the word limit

Gap-fill questions come with an instruction such as "no more than two words and/or a number". Going over the limit is marked wrong, even if the extra words are correct.

If the instruction says ONE WORD ONLY and you hear "a large wooden table", the answer is probably "table" or "wooden", depending on the sentence. Read the sentence around the gap. It often already contains some of the words you hear.

Check the grammar too. If the gap follows "an", the answer starts with a vowel sound. If it follows "two", it's plural.

3. Expect the speaker to change their mind

This is the classic Listening trap, and it appears most in Part 1.

Example: "I'd like to book for Tuesday the 12th. Oh, actually, no, I'm working that day. Can we make it Thursday the 14th?"

If you write the first thing you hear, you lose the mark. Listen for correction signals: actually, sorry, no, wait, I mean, on second thoughts, that's changed. In lessons I make students write a small arrow next to any answer that might be corrected, so they listen on for a few more seconds before moving on.

4. Use the time before each section

Before each part, you get time to read the questions. Use it to predict:

  • What type of answer: a number, a name, a date, a place, a noun?
  • What words might replace the question words. The question says "cost". The speaker may say "price", "fee" or "it'll be £20".
  • Where the questions change topic, so you know when to move on.

5. Don't chase a missed answer

Because you hear each recording once, a missed answer is gone. The real damage comes when you keep thinking about it and miss the next two as well.

Make a quick guess or leave a small mark, and move your eyes to the next question immediately. You can come back to it at the end. There's no negative marking, so a guess costs you nothing.

6. Use the transfer or check time well

On paper, the 10 minutes at the end is for transferring answers, and it's also your best chance to fix easy errors:

  • Check spelling, especially of words you wrote quickly.
  • Check plurals against the sentence grammar.
  • Check every answer is within the word limit.
  • Fill every empty box with a sensible guess.
  • Make sure answer 17 is in box 17. Misaligned answers happen more often than you would think.

On computer, you type answers as you go and only get 2 minutes at the end, so check spelling while you listen rather than relying on the end.

A practice routine

After each practice test, sort your wrong answers into groups: spelling, word limit, distractor, didn't understand, lost my place. Often the first four groups together are larger than the last one. Those are the easy marks, and they're the quickest to win back.
