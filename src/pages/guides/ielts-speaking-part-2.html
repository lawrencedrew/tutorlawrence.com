IELTS Speaking Part 2: how to talk for two minutes

Two minutes feels like a long time when you're nervous. With the right use of your one minute of preparation and a simple structure, it's very manageable.

How Part 2 works

The examiner gives you a task card with a topic and three or four prompts, such as "Describe a place you have visited that you'd like to go back to". You get one minute to prepare, with paper and a pencil for notes. Then you speak for one to two minutes without interruption. When the time is up, the examiner stops you and may ask one or two short follow-up questions.

Aim for the full two minutes. Stopping after 40 seconds gives the examiner less evidence of your English, and that usually shows in your fluency score.

Use your one minute well

Don't try to write sentences. You won't have time, and reading them out sounds unnatural. Instead, write a few keywords for each prompt on the card:

  • What and where: two or three words to set the scene.
  • A detail or story: one specific moment you can describe.
  • Feelings: how it felt, and why.
  • Why it matters: the reason you'd go back, or what it taught you.

Choose something real if you can. A real memory comes with details, and details are what keep you talking.

A structure that fills two minutes

  1. Start with the answer (15 seconds). "I'd like to talk about Kraków, which I visited for a long weekend two years ago."

  2. Set the scene (30 seconds). Who you were with, when, what it was like.

  3. Tell one specific moment (45 seconds). "One evening we walked into the old town square just as..." This is where your best vocabulary comes out naturally.

  4. Explain why (30 seconds). Why it matters to you, and why you'd go back. Finish with a clear closing sentence.

If you finish early, add an example or a comparison: "It reminded me a bit of..." or "Compared with where I live...".

Common mistakes

  • Reciting a memorised answer. Examiners recognise them, and they rarely fit the card exactly. See why memorised answers lose marks.
  • Reading the prompts back as a list. Use them as a guide, not a script. You don't have to cover them in order.
  • Stopping when the prompts run out. Keep going with an example until the examiner stops you. Being stopped is completely normal.
  • Panicking about one word. If a word won't come, describe it: "the place where you buy train tickets". That is a skill examiners reward.

How to practise

Set a one-minute timer, read a task card, make notes, then record yourself speaking for two minutes. Listen back once and note where you paused or repeated words. Do one card a day for two weeks and the two minutes will start to feel short.
