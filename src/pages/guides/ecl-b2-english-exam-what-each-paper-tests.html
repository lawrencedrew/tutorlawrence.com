ECL B2 English exam: what each paper tests

The ECL B2 exam has four modules, reading, writing, listening and speaking, each worth 25 points. There is no separate grammar paper. Here is what each module actually asks you to do, and where B2 candidates usually lose points.

The B2 exam at a glance

ModuleTimeWhat you getPoints
Reading45 min2 tasks, 800–1,000 words of text in total, 20 items25
Writing60 min2 texts of about 150 words each, each built on four bullet points25
Listening25–30 min2 recordings, 800–1,000 words in total, 20 items, each played twice25
SpeakingAbout 18–20 min, in pairsWarm-up, a guided conversation with your partner, then a talk on four pictures25

Reading and writing make up the written part. Listening and speaking make up the oral part, and the speaking test can be on a different day from the written modules.

Source: the ECL exam format page and ECL FAQ. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

What you need to pass

The rules changed in August 2026. ECL now works as a modular exam: you need at least 60% in each module, which is 15 of the 25 points. Every module you pass gets its own certificate, and if you pass all four within a year of your first exam you can ask for a complex certificate.

The old rule, 40% per skill and 60% on average, applies only to exams taken up to July 2026. If you read older advice online, check which rule it describes.

In practice this means you can no longer hide a weak skill behind a strong one. A great speaking score doesn't rescue a 12-point writing score. It does mean a bad day in one module costs you only that module, not the whole exam.

Source: ECL, Passing ECL exams. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

Reading: understanding, not vocabulary lists

You read two texts and answer 20 items. At B2 the texts can be blog posts, adverts, comments, personal letters, brochures or newspaper articles. The task types are multiple choice with three options, matching short texts to titles, statements or questions, gap-filling and short-answer questions.

Each item is worth 1.25 points, and there is no partial credit. An answer with something extra or wrong in it scores zero, so a short answer should be a few words, not a copied sentence.

What it really tests: whether you can find specific information quickly and tell the difference between what a text says and what it only seems to say. Matching tasks, in particular, are full of near-synonyms designed to tempt you.

Writing: two short texts, four bullet points each

You write two texts. Possible types include a comment or forum post, an email, a message, a report, an article or an essay. Each task gives you four bullet points to cover. A printed dictionary, monolingual or bilingual, is allowed in this module only.

Two assessors mark each text separately on five criteria, from 0 to 5 points each:

  • grammar (formal accuracy)
  • paragraphing, punctuation and spelling
  • vocabulary range
  • style and organisation
  • communicative effectiveness, which means covering the task and all four bullet points

One word count detail is worth knowing. The format page gives about 150 words per text at B2, but one of ECL's own B2 writing guides uses a 200-word task. My advice: follow the word count printed on your paper, not what you remember from a book.

Source: ECL, Criteria for assessing writing and B2 writing guide. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

Listening: two recordings, heard twice

At B2 the recordings can be conversations, reports, news, interviews, radio reports or short lectures. The questions are multiple choice with three options and short answers. You get time to read the questions before the first listening and a pause after each one.

What it really tests: whether you can follow natural speech at normal speed, including people correcting themselves and changing their minds. The answer is often the speaker's second thought, not their first.

Speaking: talking with your partner, then on your own

You take the speaking test with another candidate. After a short warm-up that isn't marked, the two of you have a guided conversation based on four bullet points. Then each of you speaks alone about a montage of four pictures. You don't get preparation time before either task. Two examiners score you on five criteria: grammar, pronunciation and fluency, vocabulary, style, and how well you complete the task.

What it really tests: interaction. In the guided conversation the examiners want to hear you and your partner talk to each other. Ask questions, react to what your partner says, and agree or disagree with reasons.

B2 or C1: which should you take?

The structure is the same at both levels. C1 has longer texts, a longer writing module with longer texts, and more abstract topics. The full C1 format is in my guide to the ECL C1 exam format, timing and scoring.

A simple test I use with students: write one 150-word B2 task in 30 minutes without a dictionary. If you finish comfortably and your text has a clear paragraph for each bullet point, try a C1 sample before deciding. If you run out of time, B2 is the safer target, especially now that you need 60% in every module.

Three things to do this week

  1. Get the official sample tests. ECL publishes a B2 sample test booklet with two complete tests, answer keys and audio.

  2. Do one reading task under time and check every wrong answer. Was it vocabulary, or did you add extra words to a short answer?

  3. Write one task and highlight the four bullet points in your text in different colours. If one colour is missing or tiny, that is your task-completion score falling.
