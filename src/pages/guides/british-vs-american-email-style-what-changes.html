British vs American email style: what changes

British and American emails share most of their rules, but they differ in directness, a few phrases, spelling and how dates are written. Here's what actually changes, and when it matters.

The big difference: how direct you are

As a general tendency, American business emails are more direct and more openly enthusiastic. British emails soften requests more and understate both good and bad news. Of course, individuals vary a lot, but the pattern is real enough to notice.

SituationTypical AmericanTypical British
AskingCan you send me the deck by Friday?Would you mind sending me the slides by Friday?
PraisingThis is awesome, great work!This looks really good, thanks.
DisagreeingI don't think that will work.I'm not sure that will quite work.
Bad newsWe have a problem with the timeline.There might be a slight issue with the timeline.
ClosingLooking forward to it!Look forward to speaking then.

The risk runs both ways. A British reader may find very direct American phrasing a little pushy. An American reader may miss how serious a British "slight issue" is. That second one matters: in British English, understatement often means the problem is bigger than the words suggest. There's more on this in understanding British understatement at work.

Greetings and sign-offs

  • Greetings: both use "Hi Anna," for everyday emails. Some American writers use a colon in formal letters ("Dear Ms Novak:"), where British writers use a comma.
  • Titles: American English usually puts a full stop after "Mr." and "Dr."; British English usually doesn't ("Mr", "Dr").
  • Sign-offs: "Best," and "Thanks," are common on both sides. "Cheers," is British and informal. "Yours faithfully" is British only; in American English, "Sincerely," is the standard formal close.
  • Exclamation marks: American emails tend to use more of them. In British emails, one per email is usually plenty.

A note on "-ize": some British publishers and organisations use "organize" too, so it isn't strictly wrong in Britain. What matters most is consistency. Pick one style and set your spell-checker to it. Mixing "organise" and "color" in one email looks careless.

Dates and times: the one that causes real problems

This is the difference that actually costs money. 04/05/2027 means 4 May in Britain and April 5 in the US.

  • British: 4 May 2027, or 04/05/2027.
  • American: May 4, 2027, or 05/04/2027.

In any email that goes across the Atlantic, or to an international team, write the month as a word: "4 May" or "May 4". Both are clear. Numbers alone are not.

Times are mostly the same, though Americans rarely use the 24-hour clock in business emails. "3pm" is safe everywhere. Add the time zone for international meetings.

And one grammar habit: British emails use the present perfect more often. A British writer says "I've just sent it"; an American often writes "I just sent it". Both are correct in their own variety. See present perfect: when British speakers actually use it for more.

Which style should you use?

Students often ask me which one is "correct". Both are. My practical advice:

  1. Follow your company. If your company has a style guide or a head office in one country, use that variety.

  2. Match your reader on tone, not spelling. With an American client, be a little more direct and positive. With a British client, soften requests a little more. Keep your own spelling consistent either way.

  3. For international teams, go for clarity. Write dates in words, avoid idioms from either side, and keep sentences short. Clear international English works with everyone.

One thing no reader on either side will mind: a short, polite email with a clear request. That matters far more than "-ise" or "-ize".
