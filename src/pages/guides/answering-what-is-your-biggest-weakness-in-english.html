Answering 'What is your biggest weakness?' in English

Name a real, minor weakness, show you know how it affects your work, and say what you're doing about it. Three steps, about 45 seconds. Here's how to phrase it so it sounds honest, not rehearsed.

Why they ask

The interviewer doesn't expect you to confess something serious. They're checking two things: self-awareness, and whether you do something about your weak points. An answer that shows both is a good answer, whatever the weakness is.

The three-step formula

  1. Name it. One real weakness, stated plainly. Not a disguised strength.

  2. Show the effect. A short, specific example of how it affected your work.

  3. Show the fix. What you're doing about it, and how it's going. Spend most of your time here.

Three sample answers

Delegation: "I've found it hard to delegate. When I moved into a team lead role, I kept doing tasks myself because it felt quicker, and I ended up working late while my team had capacity. So now I plan the week on Monday and decide who does what before I start anything. It's not perfect yet, but my team has taken on more, and I've stopped doing other people's reports at nine in the evening."

Public speaking: "Presenting to large groups doesn't come naturally to me. In small meetings I'm fine, but in front of fifty people I used to rush and read my slides. Last year I volunteered to present our monthly results internally, just to get practice, and I now prepare with notes rather than a script. I'm much calmer than I was."

Saying yes too often: "I tend to say yes to too many requests. I like being helpful, but at one point I'd agreed to support three projects and my own deadlines started slipping. Now, when someone asks, I check my workload first and give a realistic date, or I say what I'd need to drop. It's made my commitments much more reliable."

Weaknesses to avoid

  • Fake weaknesses. "I'm a perfectionist" or "I work too hard" are the most common answers in the world, and interviewers hear them as "I didn't want to answer".
  • A core skill for the job. An accountant shouldn't say "I'm not very good with numbers". Check the job description and choose something outside the essential requirements.
  • Personality flaws with no fix. "I get angry easily" raises a worry you can't solve in 45 seconds.
  • "I don't have any." It sounds like a lack of self-awareness, which is the exact thing they're testing.

Should you say your English is your weakness?

Students ask me this a lot. It can work, but only if it's true, minor, and the job doesn't depend on perfect English. Be specific. "My English" is too broad and makes the interviewer start listening for mistakes. Something like this is better:

"In fast meetings with native speakers, I sometimes take a moment longer to jump in, especially when people talk over each other. I've been working on it with a tutor, practising phrases for getting into the conversation, and I'm much more confident than I was a year ago."

That shows awareness and action, and the interviewer can hear that your English is clearly good enough. If you're working on this, see phrases to get into a meeting when nobody pauses.

Phrases that sound natural

Native speakers soften this answer. Words like "tend to" and "don't come naturally" let you admit something honestly without sounding harsh about yourself.

To name itTo show the fix
I tend to…So what I do now is…
…doesn't come naturally to me.I've been working on it by…
Something I've had to work on is…It's made a real difference to…
I've found it hard to…I'm not there yet, but…

Common English mistakes in this answer

AvoidSay instead
My weak point is that I am too much detailed.I sometimes focus too much on detail.
I am working on it since one year.I've been working on it for a year.
I have problems to delegate.I find it hard to delegate.
I improved it a lot.It's improved a lot. / I've made a lot of progress.

The second row is the one I correct most. For something that started in the past and continues now, English uses the present perfect continuous with "for" or "since". There's more on that in 'since' or 'for'? Talking about how long.

Variations of the question

The same formula works for:

  • "What would your manager say you need to improve?"
  • "What's an area of development for you?"
  • "Tell me about a time you failed." (Use the STAR method here, with the fix as your Result.)

Prepare one weakness and one backup, in case your first one doesn't suit the role or comes up earlier in the conversation.
