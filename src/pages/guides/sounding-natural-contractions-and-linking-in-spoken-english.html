Sounding natural: contractions and linking in spoken English

Native speakers don't say words one by one. They shorten small words, join the end of one word to the start of the next, and drop sounds in between. Learn a handful of these patterns and fast English becomes easier to follow and to speak.

Why "careful" English can sound unnatural

Many students speak with every word separate and every word clear: "I. Am. Going. To. Send. It. To. You." Everything is correct, but it sounds stiff, and it is tiring to listen to over a long meeting. It also works the other way: if you expect English to sound like that, real conversation between British colleagues can feel impossibly fast.

The good news is that the speed comes mostly from a few predictable habits. You don't need to copy all of them. Using some and recognising the rest is enough.

1. Use contractions when you speak

In speech, contractions are the normal form, not the casual one. Even in a formal presentation, a British speaker says "we've seen growth", not "we have seen growth".

Sounds stiff when spokenNatural
I am not sure.I'm not sure.
We will send it tomorrow.We'll send it tomorrow.
It is not ready yet.It isn't ready yet. / It's not ready yet.
I would like to add something.I'd like to add something.
There is a problem with the file.There's a problem with the file.
We should have checked.We should've checked.

A useful insight: using the full form in speech adds emphasis. "I did send it" or "It is ready" sounds like you are correcting someone. If you always speak in full forms, you can sound slightly defensive without meaning to.

One writing warning: "should've" and "would've" sound like "should of" and "would of", but those spellings are always wrong.

2. Weak forms: the small words go quiet

Grammar words like to, for, of, and, can, at, was are usually unstressed, so their vowel shrinks to a quick "uh".

  • to becomes "tuh": I need tuh check.
  • for becomes "fuh": Thanks fuh the update.
  • of becomes "uv" or just "uh": a cup uh tea, most uv them.
  • and becomes "n": salt n pepper, sales n marketing.
  • was becomes "wuz": It wuz fine.
  • can becomes "kun": I kun do Tuesday.

That last one is important in British English. "Can" is short and weak: "kun". "Can't" is long and strong, with the vowel in "car": "KAHNT". The final t is often hard to hear, so British listeners tell them apart by the vowel. If you say "can" with a full, strong vowel, it may be heard as "can't". Practise: "I kun come" against "I KAHNT come".

3. Linking: consonant into vowel

When a word ends in a consonant and the next word starts with a vowel, the consonant moves across and joins the next word.

  • turn it off: tur-ni-toff (still no r sound in "tur")
  • an email: a-nee-mail
  • pick it up: pi-ki-tup
  • send it over: sen-di-to-ver

This is why "an apple" can sound like "a napple" to learners. If you practise this one habit, your speech instantly sounds smoother.

4. The British linking r

In standard British English, an r at the end of a word is silent: "far", "our", "there", "after". But when the next word starts with a vowel, the r comes back to join them.

  • far: "fah". But far away: "fah-ra-way".
  • there on its own: "theh", no r. But there are: "theh-ra".
  • after: "ahf-tuh". But after a while: "ahf-tuh-ra-while".

You will also hear British speakers add an r where there is no letter r at all: "law and order" can sound like "lore-ran order", and "the idea of it" like "the idea-ruv it". You don't need to copy this, but knowing it exists stops it confusing you.

5. Gliding between vowels

When one word ends in a vowel and the next starts with one, a tiny "w" or "y" sound appears to join them.

  • go on: go-won. Do it: do-wit.
  • I agree: I-yagree. The end: the-yend.

Without this glide, speakers often put a small stop between the words, which makes the speech sound choppy.

6. Sounds that disappear

A t or d squeezed between two other consonants often vanishes:

  • next week: "nex week"
  • last month: "lass month"
  • I don't know: "I dunno" in relaxed speech
  • handbag: "hambag"

And in fast speech you joins the word before it: "did you" sounds like "didja", "would you" like "wouldja", "what do you think" like "whaddya think". Many British speakers also replace a t with a little catch in the throat, so "not a lot" can sound like "no' a lo'". These are for listening, not for copying in a job interview.

How to practise without overdoing it

Take three sentences you say often at work, for example "I'll send it over this afternoon", "Can I add something?" and "Let's pick it up next week". Mark the links with a pencil: I'll-sen-di-to-ver. Say them slowly with the links, then at normal speed. Record yourself and compare with how a British colleague would say it.

Shadowing, where you play a short clip and speak along with it a split second behind, is the fastest way to absorb these patterns.
