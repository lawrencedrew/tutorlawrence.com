Writing a cover letter in English for a UK employer

Keep it to one page, write it for this job only, and back up every claim with an example. A UK cover letter is shorter, plainer and more direct than in many European countries. Here's the structure and the language.

What a UK employer expects

  • One page, usually less. Three to five short paragraphs is typical.
  • Specific to the job. It should mention the role, and ideally the reference number if there is one.
  • Evidence, not adjectives. Show what you've done that matches what they need.
  • Plain, confident tone. Polite, but not ceremonial.

Official UK careers advice suggests keeping it short and to the point, ideally one A4 page, saying what the vacancy is and how you heard about it, and listing the skills you have that are relevant to the job.

Source: nidirect, CVs and cover letters. Check it before you apply in case anything has changed.

The structure, paragraph by paragraph

  1. Opening: the job you're applying for and one sentence on why you're a strong fit.

  2. Evidence 1: your most relevant experience, matched to the most important requirement in the advert.

  3. Evidence 2: a second requirement, with a concrete example.

  4. Why them: one or two sentences on why this company or role, with a real detail.

  5. Close: a short, positive ending.

A short example

Dear Ms Patel,

I'm writing to apply for the role of Operations Analyst (ref. OA-214), advertised on your website. I have five years' experience in supply chain operations, and I've spent the last two building the reporting that my current team uses to plan stock.

Your advert asks for strong Excel and data skills. In my current role, I created a weekly dashboard that brings together data from three systems, which has replaced a manual process that used to take a full day each week.

You also mention working with suppliers across Europe. I deal with suppliers in Germany, Poland and Italy every week, mostly in English, and I'm comfortable handling delays and renegotiating delivery dates.

I'm particularly interested in this role because of your plan to open a new distribution centre next year. I'd enjoy helping to set up processes from the start.

I'd welcome the chance to discuss the role further. Thank you for your time.

Yours sincerely,
Marta Kowalska

That's under 200 words. It answers the advert point by point, and every claim has an example behind it.

Greetings and sign-offs

If you know the nameIf you don't
Dear Ms Patel, … Yours sincerely,Dear Hiring Manager, … Yours faithfully,
Dear Tom Clarke, (if unsure of title) … Yours sincerely,Dear Sir or Madam, … Yours faithfully, (more traditional)

In British English, "Ms", "Mr" and "Dr" have no full stop. "Kind regards" is also widely used, especially when you're sending the letter in the body of an email. Try to find a name: a named letter feels more personal. For more on openings and closings generally, see opening and closing lines for English emails.

Phrases that sound translated or too formal

AvoidWrite instead
I would like to express my interest in applying for the position of…I'm writing to apply for the role of…
I am a dynamic, motivated and hard-working person.(Delete it. Show these qualities with an example.)
I have a big experience in…I have extensive experience in… / I've worked in … for six years.
I am sending you my CV in attachment.I've attached my CV.
I remain at your disposal for any further information.I'd be happy to provide any further information.
Hoping for a positive answer.I look forward to hearing from you.

Contractions like "I'm" and "I've" are fine in a modern UK cover letter, especially for business and tech roles. If you're applying to a very traditional sector, like law or the civil service, you might prefer the full forms. Either is acceptable. Just be consistent.

The tip most people miss: mirror the advert

UK job adverts often list "essential" and "desirable" criteria. Use the same words in your letter. If the advert says "stakeholder management", write "stakeholder management", not "working with different people". Many employers screen applications against those criteria, sometimes with software, and a recruiter skimming quickly is looking for exactly those phrases.

Go through the advert with a highlighter, pick the three most important requirements, and make sure each one appears in your letter with an example.

Things to leave out

  • Your life story. Start with the job, not with where you went to school.
  • Salary, unless the advert asks for it.
  • Negatives, such as why you're leaving your current job.
  • Repeating your CV. The letter explains why your experience fits. The CV lists it.

Before you send it

  • Check the company name and the job title are correct. A letter that names a different company is the fastest way to be rejected.
  • Read it aloud. If a sentence is hard to say, it's probably hard to read.
  • Use British spelling if it's a UK employer: organise, colour, programme, centre.
