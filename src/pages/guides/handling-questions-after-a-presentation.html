Handling questions after a presentation

The Q&A is the part you can't script, so it's the part people fear most. But almost every answer follows the same four moves: thank, check, answer briefly, confirm. Learn the phrases for each move and you'll stay calm whatever comes.

Why the Q&A is easier than it looks

You can't know the exact questions, but you can predict most of them. And the language around your answers is fixed. Native speakers use the same handful of phrases to accept a question, give themselves thinking time and finish their answer. If those phrases are automatic, your brain is free to think about the content.

Before you present: predict five questions

This is the most useful preparation you can do, and most people skip it. Write down the five questions you'd least like to be asked. Usually they're about cost, timing, risk, "why not the other option?" and the one weak number in your data. Prepare a two-sentence answer to each and say it out loud once. When one of them comes up, you'll sound like you've thought it through, because you have.

Move 1: accept the question

  • "Thanks, that's a good question."
  • "Good point. Thanks for raising it."
  • "Yes, I'm glad you asked that."

Don't say "good question" to every single person. Once or twice is natural. Every time sounds automatic. You can also just say "Sure" or "Yes" and go straight into the answer.

Move 2: check you've understood

If the question was long, unclear or hard to hear, check it before you answer. This is not a weakness. It's what experienced presenters do, and it buys you thinking time.

  • "So if I understand correctly, you're asking whether…"
  • "Just to check, do you mean the cost for this year or overall?"
  • "Sorry, could you say that again? I didn't quite catch the last part."

Rephrasing the question also helps the rest of the room, especially on a video call where not everyone heard it. More phrases for this are in how to ask someone to repeat or slow down.

Move 3: answer briefly

Give the direct answer in your first sentence, then one reason or example. Stop there. A short answer sounds confident. A long one sounds like you're not sure.

Too long and unclearShort and clear
Well, there are many factors, and it depends on the market, and we looked at several options last year, and in some ways yes but also…Yes, we expect costs to rise slightly. The main reason is the new supplier contract, which starts in March.

If you need a second to think, use a thinking phrase instead of silence or "eeeh": "Let me think about that for a moment." "That's an interesting one." "Well, the short answer is…" These sound natural and calm. There are more in phrases for buying time to think.

Move 4: check they're satisfied

  • "Does that answer your question?"
  • "I hope that helps."
  • "Is that what you were asking?"

This closes the answer clearly and hands control back to you. Then: "Any other questions?"

When you don't know the answer

Never guess. Saying you don't know, politely, is completely normal in British business culture. Making something up is not.

  • "I'm afraid I don't have that figure with me, but I can find out and email it to you."
  • "That's outside my area, but Kasia in finance would know. I'll put you in touch."
  • "I'd rather not guess. Let me check and get back to you by the end of the week."

The important part is the promise and the timeframe. "I'll get back to you" with no date sounds like you'll forget. "By Friday" sounds professional.

Difficult moments

A question that's really a long comment: "Thanks, that's a useful perspective. Is there a specific question you'd like me to answer?"

A question that's off topic: "That's a good question, but it's a bit outside today's topic. Could we talk about it afterwards?"

Someone who disagrees with you: "I can see why you'd say that. Our view is slightly different, because…" Acknowledge first, then give your reason. The guide on how to disagree politely in English has the full structure.

Silence, nobody asks anything: "One question I often get is… " Then answer it yourself. It restarts the room and often encourages a real question.

Closing the Q&A

  • "I think we've got time for one more question."
  • "OK, I'm conscious of time, so let's stop there. Thanks for all the questions."
  • "If you think of anything else, feel free to email me."

A tip from lessons: answer the person, then the room

When someone asks a question, look at them while you listen and start your answer. Then, halfway through, turn to the whole room. It sounds like a small thing, but it stops the Q&A becoming a private conversation, and it makes you look in control even when the question was a difficult one.
