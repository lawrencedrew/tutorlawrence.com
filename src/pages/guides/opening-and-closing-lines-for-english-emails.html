Opening and closing lines for English emails

The first and last lines of an email set its tone. Here are the greetings, openers, closing lines and sign-offs I recommend, sorted from formal to friendly, with the ones to avoid.

Greetings

GreetingWhen to use it
Dear Ms Kowalska,First contact, formal situations, senior people you don't know
Dear Anna,Polite but personal: clients you know, formal requests to colleagues
Hi Anna,The everyday default for most work emails
Hello Anna,Slightly more neutral than "Hi"; fine anywhere
Hi all, / Hi everyone,Emails to a group
Anna,Just the name: very short, can feel cold unless you know each other well

A few rules worth knowing:

  • In British English, "Mr", "Mrs", "Ms" and "Dr" usually have no full stop.
  • Use "Ms" for a woman unless you know she prefers "Mrs". It's the safe, standard choice.
  • Use the surname with a title ("Dear Mr Weber"), never the first name ("Dear Mr Thomas" when his first name is Thomas).
  • "Dear Sir or Madam" is only for when you have no name at all. A quick search for the right person's name is usually worth it.
  • Mirror the other person. If they reply "Hi Anna" and sign with their first name, you can do the same.

First lines

The first line should either connect to something or say why you're writing.

Replying

  • Thanks for your email.
  • Thanks for getting back to me.
  • Thanks for sending this over.
  • Thanks for your quick reply. (when it really was quick)

Starting a new email

  • I'm writing about…
  • I'm writing to ask whether…
  • Just a quick note to let you know…
  • Following our call this morning, …
  • I'm [name], and I'm responsible for… at [company]. (first contact)

Friendly warm-up lines (optional)

  • I hope you're well.
  • I hope you had a good weekend. (Monday or Tuesday only)
  • Good to see you at the conference last week.

"I hope you're well" is fine, but use it with people you don't email every day. With a close colleague, five emails a day each starting "I hope you're well" begins to sound robotic.

First lines to avoid

AvoidUse
I am writing you this email to inform that…I'm writing to let you know that…
Hope you are fine.I hope you're well.
Referring to your email…Thanks for your email about…
Good morning (in an email read in the afternoon)Hi / Hello

"Hope you are fine" is understandable but sounds translated. "I hope you're well" or "I hope all is well" is what a British colleague would write.

Closing lines

The line before your sign-off should tell the reader what happens next, or offer help.

  • Let me know if you have any questions.
  • Could you let me know by Friday?
  • I'll send the final version on Monday.
  • Thanks again for your help with this.
  • I look forward to hearing from you. (formal, good for first contact and job applications)
  • Speak soon. (friendly, with colleagues)

Avoid "Waiting for your answer" and "I remain at your disposal". The first sounds impatient and the second sounds very old-fashioned. There are more of these in email phrases that sound translated.

Sign-offs

Sign-offTone and use
Yours faithfully,Very formal, after "Dear Sir or Madam" only
Yours sincerely,Formal letters and emails starting with a name ("Dear Ms Kowalska")
Kind regards,Polite and safe for almost any work email
Best regards,Same as "Kind regards", very common internationally
Best wishes,Warm, slightly personal
Thanks, / Many thanks,When you've asked for something
Best,Short and friendly, common with colleagues
Cheers,Informal British; only with people you know well

The "faithfully / sincerely" rule is the classic British one, and it still matters in formal letters and job applications. In everyday email, "Kind regards" covers almost everything.

A detail most people miss: the reply chain

As a conversation goes on, greetings and sign-offs usually get shorter. The first email is "Dear Anna … Kind regards, Tom". By the fourth reply, it may be "Hi Anna … Thanks, Tom", or even no greeting at all in a quick back-and-forth. That's not rude; it's normal. What can sound cold is going the other way: suddenly switching from "Hi Anna" back to "Dear Ms Kowalska" signals that something is wrong. If you need to become formal mid-thread, for a complaint for example, do it on purpose.

Two complete combinations to copy

Formal: "Dear Mr Becker, I'm writing about the proposal you sent on 3 March. … I look forward to hearing from you. Kind regards, Julia Novak"

Everyday: "Hi Max, thanks for sending this over. … Let me know if you have any questions. Best, Julia"
