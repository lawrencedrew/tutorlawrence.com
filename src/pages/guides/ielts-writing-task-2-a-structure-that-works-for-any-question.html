IELTS Writing Task 2: a structure that works for any question

You don't need a different template for every question type. One four-paragraph shape, adjusted slightly for each type, is enough. Most of your marks come from answering the exact question inside it.

What Task 2 asks of you

Task 2 is an essay of at least 250 words responding to a point of view, an argument or a problem. The recommended time is 40 minutes out of the 60-minute Writing test. It also counts for twice as much of your Writing score as Task 1, so it deserves more of your preparation time too.

It's marked on four equally weighted criteria: Task Response, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy. A clear structure does a lot of work for the first two before you have written a single impressive word.

Source: the British Council's IELTS Writing test format page. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

The four-paragraph shape

  1. Introduction (2 sentences). Paraphrase the question in your own words, then give your answer or say what the essay will do.

  2. Body paragraph 1 (5–6 sentences). One main idea. Topic sentence, explanation, example, and a sentence linking it back to the question.

  3. Body paragraph 2 (5–6 sentences). A second main idea, built the same way.

  4. Conclusion (1–2 sentences). Restate your position clearly. No new ideas.

That comes out at roughly 270–310 words, which is a comfortable length. Writing far more rarely helps. It mostly gives you more chances to make mistakes and less time to check.

How the shape adapts to each question type

The paragraphs stay the same. What changes is the job each body paragraph does.

Question typeBody paragraph 1Body paragraph 2
To what extent do you agree?Your strongest reasonYour second reason, or the other side and why it is weaker
Discuss both views and give your opinionView A, explained fairlyView B, explained fairly, then which you prefer and why
Problems and solutions / causes and effectsThe problems (or causes)The solutions (or effects), matched to the problems
Advantages outweigh disadvantages?The weaker sideThe stronger side, and why it wins
Two-part questionAnswer to question 1Answer to question 2

Notice that in "discuss both views" you still give your own opinion. Leaving it out is one of the most common reasons a sensible essay stays at band 6 for Task Response.

A worked example

Question: Some people think that working from home is better for employees, while others believe it harms their careers. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Introduction: "Many companies now allow staff to work remotely, and opinion is divided on whether this benefits employees or holds them back. While remote work offers real advantages, I believe that, without deliberate effort, it can slow a person's progress."

Body 1, topic sentence: "Supporters of home working point mainly to the time and money saved on commuting."

Body 2, topic sentence: "On the other hand, employees who are rarely seen in the office may miss the informal contact that leads to promotion."

Conclusion: "In my view, working from home suits many people, but those who choose it need to make an extra effort to stay visible."

The opinion appears in the introduction and again in the conclusion, and the two match. That consistency is exactly what the examiner looks for when deciding whether your position is clear throughout.

The topic sentence does most of the work

When I mark essays, the paragraph that loses marks is usually one that starts well and then drifts. The writer explains one idea, then adds a second and a third, and the paragraph ends somewhere new. A simple fix is to write the topic sentence first and check every other sentence against it. If a sentence doesn't support that one idea, cut it or move it.

Build each body paragraph like this:

  • Point: "One major benefit is…"
  • Explain: "This is because…" / "As a result…"
  • Example: "For instance, an employee who…"
  • Link back: "This suggests that home working can…"

The example doesn't have to be a statistic. Examiners aren't checking facts. A short, realistic situation works well and is easier to write under time pressure.

How to spend your 40 minutes

  • 5 minutes planning. Underline the question words, decide your opinion, and write one topic sentence for each body paragraph.
  • 30 minutes writing. Roughly 3 for the introduction, 12 for each body paragraph, 3 for the conclusion.
  • 5 minutes checking. Look for your own typical errors: articles, plural -s, verb agreement.

Five minutes of planning feels like a luxury in the exam. In practice it's the part students most often skip and the part that saves most essays.

Mistakes the structure won't fix by itself

  • Copying the question. The official band descriptors say copied wording from the question is discounted, so it shows the examiner nothing about your English. Paraphrase it.
  • Memorised openings. "In this day and age, it is a controversial issue that…" says nothing and wastes your time.
  • Answering a nearby question. If the question asks about young people, an essay about "people" in general only partly answers it.
  • Too many linkers. "Firstly, moreover, furthermore" at the start of every sentence reads as mechanical. See linking words that examiners actually reward.
