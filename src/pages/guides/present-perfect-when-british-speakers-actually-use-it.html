Present perfect: when British speakers actually use it

British speakers use the present perfect when the past is connected to now, and the past simple when it is finished and dated. Once you see the connection to "now", most of the confusion goes away.

The one question to ask

Before you choose a tense, ask: am I talking about a finished moment, or about now?

  • I sent the report yesterday. A finished moment. Past simple.
  • I've sent the report. The news is about now: it's in your inbox. Present perfect.

Both sentences describe the same action. The tense shows what the speaker is interested in. That is why textbook rules ("use the present perfect for experience, for unfinished time…") can feel slippery: they are all versions of this one idea.

Four situations where British speakers use it

1. News and updates

When you announce something new that matters now, use the present perfect. This is the most common use in meetings and emails.

  • I've finished the slides.
  • The client has cancelled.
  • We've had some feedback from the board.

2. With just, already and yet

In British English these words normally go with the present perfect.

  • I've just spoken to Tom.
  • Have you sent the invoice yet?
  • She's already approved it.

Americans often use the past simple here: Did you send the invoice yet? I just spoke to Tom. That is correct American English and British people understand it easily. But if you are aiming for British English, or writing for a British company, the present perfect sounds more natural.

3. Situations that started in the past and continue

I've worked here since 2020. We've used this supplier for years. If the situation is still true, it's present perfect. There is a full guide on this in since or for.

4. Experience, with no time given

I've managed teams of up to twenty people. Have you ever worked with a German client? The question isn't when, it's whether, so no date is needed. This is useful in interviews, because it lets you list experience without telling the interviewer exactly how long ago it was.

When British speakers switch to the past simple

As soon as a finished time is mentioned or understood, the past simple takes over. These words almost always mean past simple:

yesterday, last week, in 2019, two days ago, on Monday, at the meeting, when I was at university, then

"When" questions are a common trap. "When" asks for a finished moment, so it takes the past simple.

The pattern that native speakers use all the time

This is the insight I find most useful in lessons. In real conversation, British speakers often open with the present perfect and then switch to the past simple for the details.

"I've lost my laptop charger. I think I left it in the meeting room on Tuesday."

"Have you seen the new pricing? Finance sent it round this morning."

"We've had a complaint from the Leeds office. They said the delivery arrived damaged."

The first sentence is the headline, connected to now. Everything after it tells the story, which is finished. If you keep using the present perfect for every sentence, it sounds odd. If you never use it, your news sounds like old history. The switch is what makes it sound natural.

Grey areas

"Today", "this week", "this morning"

It depends on whether the period is finished. At 10 am: I've had three calls this morning. At 4 pm, the morning is over: I had three calls this morning. "This week" and "this year" are still running, so I've been in London twice this week.

Been or gone

She's gone to Paris means she is there now. She's been to Paris means she went and came back, or that she has visited at some point in her life. In the office: "Where's Mark?" "He's gone to lunch."

"It's the first time…"

After "It's the first time", English uses the present perfect: It's the first time I've presented to the board. Many languages use the present here, so "It's the first time I present" is a very common mistake.

A quick self-check for your next email

Look at each past verb in your next update email and ask two things. Is there a finished time in the sentence? Use the past simple. Is it news that matters now, with no time? Use the present perfect. Then check your first line: I've attached the revised plan is a better start than I attached the revised plan, because the reader cares that it is there now.
