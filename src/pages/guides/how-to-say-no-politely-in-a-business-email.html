How to say no politely in a business email

In English, a polite no has four parts: thanks, the no, a short reason and something positive. Get those in the right order and you can decline almost anything without damaging the relationship.

Why "no" is harder in English than you think

English speakers, and British speakers in particular, rarely say a bare "no" at work. A short "No, we can't do this." can read as angry, even if you meant it neutrally. On the other hand, if you soften too much, the reader may think you said yes. I see both problems in lessons every week.

The goal is a message that is unmistakably a no, but warm. The structure below does that.

The four-step structure

  1. Thank them. "Thanks for thinking of us." / "Thank you for the invitation." / "Thanks for sending this over."

  2. Say no clearly. "Unfortunately, we won't be able to…" / "I'm afraid I can't…"

  3. Give a short reason. One sentence. "…as the team is fully booked until March."

  4. Offer something. An alternative, a later date, another person, or simply good wishes.

The reason should be short and true. You don't owe a long explanation, and a long one invites negotiation.

Phrases for the "no" itself

  • Unfortunately, we won't be able to take this on.
  • I'm afraid I can't make it on Thursday.
  • I'm sorry, but that won't be possible this time.
  • We've decided not to go ahead with the proposal.
  • I don't think I'm the right person for this. (useful when someone else is)
  • I'll have to pass on this one. (informal, with colleagues)

"I'm afraid" here doesn't mean fear. It is a softener that means "I'm sorry to say". It's one of the most useful two words in British business English.

Phrases for the positive ending

  • Could we look at this again in the new year?
  • You might want to contact Sara in our Madrid office, who handles these projects.
  • What I can do is send you the draft by Friday instead.
  • Please keep us in mind for future projects.
  • I hope the event goes well.

Examples for common situations

Declining extra work from a colleague

"Hi Jan, thanks for asking me. I'm afraid I can't take on the report this week, as I'm finishing the client audit by Friday. I could help next week if that's not too late, or Kasia might have time now."

Declining a supplier's proposal

"Dear Ms Hart, thank you for your detailed proposal and for your time last week. After reviewing all the offers, we've decided to go with another supplier on this occasion. We were impressed by your team, and we'd be glad to invite you to tender again next year."

Declining a meeting invitation

"Hi Leo, thanks for the invite. Unfortunately, I'm out of the office on the 14th. Could you send me the notes afterwards? I'll add any comments by the end of the week."

Declining a discount request

"Hi Marco, thanks for coming back to us. I'm afraid we can't reduce the price further, as it already includes our volume discount. What we can do is extend the payment terms to 45 days, if that helps."

Too blunt, too soft, just right

Too bluntToo soft (sounds like maybe)Just right
No, we can't do it.It might be a bit difficult, but let's see.Unfortunately, we can't do it this month.
I don't have time.I'll try to find some time.I'm afraid I don't have capacity this week.
Your price is too high.We'll think about it.We've decided not to go ahead at this price.

The middle column is a real trap. "Let's see" and "I'll try" mean no to a British speaker, but many international readers take them literally and wait for a yes. If you mean no, say it clearly, then soften the sentences around it.

What not to do

  • Don't over-apologise. One "sorry" or "unfortunately" is enough. Three apologies make it sound like you did something wrong.
  • Don't invent excuses. If they're not true, they tend to cause problems later.
  • Don't leave it unanswered. Silence is the least polite no of all.
  • Don't hide the no in the last line. Put it in the first or second sentence after your thanks.

A teacher's tip: say "no" to the task, not the person

Compare "I can't help you" with "I can't take this on this week". The first is about the person. The second is about the task and the time. Keeping the "no" focused on the task, and the warmth focused on the person, is what makes declining feel natural in English.
