Video interviews in English: how to prepare

A video interview is harder to follow than a face-to-face one, especially in a second language. Fix your sound first, learn five phrases for when the connection fails, and slow down a little. Here's the full checklist.

Why video is harder in English

On a call, you lose some of the things that help you understand: lip movements are slightly out of sync, voices are compressed, and there's often a small delay. Native speakers find this tiring too. For a non-native speaker, it can be the difference between following easily and missing every third word.

I teach all my lessons on video, and the pattern is very clear: when the sound is good, people's English sounds better. So most of your preparation should go into sound, not lighting.

The setup checklist

  1. Use headphones with a microphone. Wired earphones are fine. This is the single biggest improvement you can make. Laptop microphones pick up the room, echo and your keyboard.

  2. Test on the actual platform. Teams, Zoom and Google Meet all behave differently. If the invitation uses Teams, test Teams, ideally a day before, and check you can log in without a work account if needed.

  3. Choose a quiet room with soft things in it. Curtains, a sofa and a bookshelf reduce echo. An empty room with hard walls sounds like a bathroom.

  4. Put the camera at eye level. Stack the laptop on books. Looking down into a camera is unflattering and looks less confident.

  5. Light from the front. Face a window or a lamp. A window behind you turns you into a silhouette.

  6. Close everything else. Notifications, other tabs, and any app that might start updating.

  7. Have a phone backup. Note the interviewer's phone number or email, so you can contact them if the call drops.

Notes on screen: the honest advice

One of the advantages of video is that you can keep a few notes nearby. Use this carefully. A sticky note beside the camera with three key words for each STAR story is helpful. A full script is not, because your eyes move as you read and your voice goes flat. Interviewers notice quickly.

If you've prepared answers using the STAR method, a single line per story is enough to remind you.

Five phrases for when things go wrong

Something will go wrong at some point. What matters is how calmly you deal with it. These phrases sound natural and professional:

ProblemWhat to say
The sound breaks up"Sorry, you broke up for a moment there. Could you repeat the last part?"
You both speak at once"Sorry, go ahead." (Then let them finish.)
They freeze"I think you've frozen. I'll wait a moment."
You're not sure they heard you"Did that come through clearly?"
The call dropsRejoin immediately, then: "Sorry about that, I think the connection dropped. Shall I start that answer again?"

Notice that "you broke up" blames the connection, not the person's accent or your English. That's what makes it comfortable to say. There are more in phrases for when the line is bad.

When you didn't understand the question

This is different from a bad connection, and it's fine to say so. Don't guess. A wrong answer to a misheard question is far worse than a short clarification:

  • "Just to check I've understood, are you asking about…?"
  • "Could you rephrase that slightly?"
  • "Do you mean in my current role, or in general?"

Checking the question shows care, not weakness. For the moments when you understood but need a second to think, see interview phrases for buying time to think.

How to speak on a video call

  • Slow down slightly. Nerves make everyone speak faster, and compression makes fast speech harder to follow. A little slower than normal sounds calm, not slow.
  • Pause at the end of answers. Because of the delay, if you finish and immediately add more, you'll talk over the next question. Finish, pause, and let them speak.
  • Make your endings clear. On video, the final sounds of words like "asked", "tests" and "months" disappear easily. Pronounce them fully.
  • Look at the camera when you make your key point. You can't do it all the time, but looking at the lens for your most important sentence feels like eye contact to the interviewer.

Recorded (one-way) video interviews

Some employers send a link where you record answers to questions on screen, with no interviewer present. Usually you get a short time to think and a time limit to answer. Read the instructions carefully: some platforms allow a practice question or a retake and others don't.

The trick here is to imagine a real person behind the camera. Talking to a blank screen makes most people sound stiff. Smile at the start, use the person's company name, and keep an eye on the timer so you can finish your sentence rather than being cut off.

The day before

Do a ten-minute test call with a friend on the same platform, in the same room, with the same headphones. Ask them honestly: "Can you hear me clearly? Am I speaking too fast?" That one call will remove most of your nerves on the day.
