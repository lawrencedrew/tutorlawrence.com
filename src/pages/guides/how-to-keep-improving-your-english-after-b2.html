How to keep improving your English after B2

At B2 your English works, so the pressure to improve disappears and progress stalls. Moving on means changing what you practise: less new grammar, more precision, more natural phrases, and honest feedback on what you produce.

Why progress slows at B2

Up to B2, every week brings obvious progress: new tenses, new topics, new situations you can handle. Then you reach a point where you can do your job in English. Colleagues understand you. Nobody corrects you any more, because your mistakes don't stop communication.

That is exactly the problem. Your brain has found a system that works, and it stops looking for a better one. Many professionals I teach describe the same feeling: "I'm not getting worse, but I'm not getting better either." It is often called the intermediate plateau, and it is completely normal.

What actually changes between B2 and C1

The Council of Europe's level descriptions are useful here. At B2, a learner can interact "with a degree of fluency and spontaneity that makes regular interaction with native speakers quite possible". At C1, they can express themselves "fluently and spontaneously without much obvious searching for expressions", "recognise implicit meaning", and "use language flexibly and effectively for social, academic and professional purposes".

Source: the Council of Europe's Common European Framework of Reference, global scale. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

Notice what is not on that list: harder grammar. The jump to C1 is mostly about flexibility, precision and reading between the lines. That changes how you should study.

1. Stop learning words. Learn combinations

A B2 speaker says "a big problem", "make a decision quickly", "very important". A C1 speaker says "a major setback", "reach a decision", "absolutely crucial". The words aren't rarer. They are the combinations native speakers expect.

When you read something in your field, underline word partnerships, not new words: "raise a concern", "meet a deadline", "a steep rise", "broadly agree". Then try to use them. My guide to active vocabulary has a routine for this.

2. Fix your fossilised mistakes

Every B2 speaker has a few errors they have made thousands of times. They are so automatic you no longer hear them. Common ones: "I am agree", "discuss about", "since three years", "the informations".

Here is the practical insight: these errors almost never disappear through more practice, because practice repeats them. They need someone to point them out and then a deliberate period of conscious correction. Record five minutes of yourself speaking, transcribe a section, and look for patterns. Or ask someone to note your errors without interrupting. See "I am agree" and other mistakes even advanced speakers make for the most common ones.

3. Learn to change register

At B2, people often have one style for everything. At C1, you shift depending on who you are talking to:

  • To a client: "I'm afraid we won't be able to meet the original deadline."
  • To your manager: "We're going to miss the deadline, I'm afraid."
  • To a close colleague: "Yeah, there's no way we're hitting that date."

Same message, three registers. Practise by taking one email you sent this week and rewriting it twice: once more formal, once more relaxed.

4. Read and listen above your comfort zone

If you only read work emails and watch series with subtitles in your own language, your input is stuck at the level you already have. Choose material that makes you work a little: long-form journalism, opinion columns, industry reports, podcasts with fast conversation. Opinion writing is especially good for C1 because it is full of implied meaning, irony and hedging, exactly the skills on that C1 list.

5. Push your output, not just your input

Reading more won't fix your speaking. You need to produce language at the edge of your ability and get feedback. Some ways to do it:

  • Reformulation. Write a paragraph, have a proficient speaker rewrite it naturally, and compare the two line by line. Every difference is a lesson.
  • Retelling. Read or listen to something, then explain it out loud in two minutes without notes. Then do it again, aiming to be more precise.
  • Upgrading. After a meeting, write down one thing you said simply and how you could have said it better.

6. Set a concrete goal

"Get better at English" is too vague to motivate you at B2. Better goals: "lead the Monday meeting in English by March", "write client proposals without a native speaker checking them", "pass the C1 exam in June". A specific goal tells you what to practise, and it gives you a way to see progress. An exam can work well for this; see which English exam to take if you are considering one.

How long it takes

Honestly, the move from B2 to C1 is slower than earlier levels, because the gains are smaller and harder to notice. Progress will feel less dramatic. But if you are deliberately working on precision, register and your fixed errors, the change is real, and colleagues often notice it before you do.
