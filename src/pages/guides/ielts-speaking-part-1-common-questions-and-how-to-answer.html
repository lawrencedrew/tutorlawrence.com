IELTS Speaking Part 1: common questions and how to answer naturally

Part 1 is a short, friendly interview about you. The best answers are two or three sentences: a direct answer, a reason or detail, and then you stop.

How Part 1 works

Part 1 lasts 4–5 minutes. The examiner introduces themselves, checks your identity, and then asks general questions on familiar topics such as home, family, work, studies and interests. The whole Speaking test takes 11–14 minutes and is recorded.

You're marked on the same four criteria throughout the test: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. Part 1 is where the examiner forms a first impression, so a relaxed, natural start helps.

Sources: IELTS Speaking format on ielts.org and the British Council's Speaking test format page. Check them before your exam in case anything has changed.

The right length

The examiner has several short topics to get through in a few minutes. So Part 1 answers should be short but not minimal. Too short ("Yes, I do.") gives the examiner nothing to assess. Too long and they'll interrupt you to move on, which isn't a problem in itself but can throw you off.

A good shape: answer + reason or detail + (optional) a small extra. That's usually 15 to 25 seconds.

Too shortAbout right
Yes, I like it.Yes, I really enjoy it, mostly because no two days are the same. This week, for example, I've been working on a project with our team in Spain.
No, not really.Not really, to be honest. I used to cook a lot, but these days I'm usually too tired after work, so it's mostly something quick.

Common topics, with natural answers

Work or studies

"What do you do?" "I'm a project coordinator at a logistics company in Gdańsk. I mainly organise deliveries and deal with clients, so I spend a lot of my day on the phone."

"Do you enjoy your job?" "Most of the time, yes. I like the variety, although it can get a bit stressful at the end of the month when everything's due at once."

Home and hometown

"Do you live in a house or a flat?" "A flat, in the centre of town. It's quite small, but it's only ten minutes from my office, which I really appreciate."

"What do you like about your hometown?" "Probably the old town. It's not huge, but it's lovely in the evenings, and there are some great little restaurants there."

Free time and interests

"What do you do in your free time?" "I try to get outside as much as possible. I go running a couple of times a week, and at weekends we often go walking in the hills."

Everyday topics

The official list says "familiar topics such as" home, family, work, studies and interests, so be ready for other everyday subjects too: food, transport, music, your phone, shopping. Questions like these are usually simple: "Do you prefer…?", "How often do you…?", "Did you… when you were a child?"

"Did you enjoy music lessons as a child?" "Not especially. We had to learn the recorder at school and I was terrible at it. I enjoy listening to music much more than playing it."

Listen for the tense

This is the small detail I point out most in Part 1 mocks. The question tells you which tense to use, and switching tense is an easy way to show grammatical range without trying.

  • "Do you…?" → present simple: "Yes, I usually…"
  • "Did you… as a child?" → past simple: "I did, actually. I used to…"
  • "Have you ever…?" → present perfect: "I have, once. I went…"
  • "Would you like to…?" → would: "I'd love to, if I had the time."

Students often answer "Did you…?" questions in the present. It's a small error, but examiners notice it.

Natural ways to start an answer

Native speakers rarely start with "Yes" or "No" alone. These sound natural and give you a second to think:

  • "Yes, definitely…" / "Not really, to be honest…"
  • "It depends, really…"
  • "I'd say so, yes…"
  • "Actually, I…" (when your answer is a bit unexpected)
  • "Hmm, I haven't thought about that, but…"

Avoid starting every answer with "Well," or repeating the question back in full: "Do I like cooking? I like cooking because…" It sounds rehearsed.

Things to avoid

  • Memorised answers. Part 1 questions are short and specific, and a prepared paragraph rarely fits. See why memorised answers lose marks.
  • Over-formal language. "I reside in an apartment" sounds odd. "I live in a flat" is natural.
  • Worrying about the truth. You can simplify. If your real job takes five minutes to explain, give a one-sentence version.

How to practise

Write a list of 20 Part 1 questions across the topics above. Ask a friend or record yourself answering them in a random order, with no preparation. Listen back and check: did each answer have a detail or reason? Did you use the right tense? Then move on to Part 2 and Part 3.
