Make or do? The rule and the exceptions

"Make" is usually for creating something, and "do" is for carrying out an activity. That rule gets you most of the way. The rest is a short list of fixed phrases, and the ones you need at work are below.

The basic rule

Many languages have one verb where English has two, so this choice feels random. It isn't completely random. Start with this:

  • Make = produce, create or cause something. At the end, something exists that didn't before. Make a cake, make a plan, make a copy, make a mess.
  • Do = perform an activity, task or job. The focus is on the action, not a result. Do the shopping, do your job, do some research.

There is a second test that helps: "do" is the general verb for unnamed activity. What are you doing this weekend? I've got a lot to do. Do something. You don't use "make" in that vague way.

Work phrases with "make"

These are the ones I hear most often with "do" by mistake. Learn them as fixed pairs.

  • make a decision (not "do a decision")
  • make a mistake
  • make a call (phone a person, and also "make the call" meaning decide)
  • make progress
  • make an effort
  • make an offer / a proposal / a suggestion
  • make a complaint
  • make an appointment / arrangements
  • make money / a profit / a loss
  • make a point (state an argument in a discussion)
  • make changes
  • make sure and make sense

Some of these fit the rule (a profit and a proposal are results). Others, like "make a decision" and "make an effort", just have to be memorised.

Work phrases with "do"

  • do business (with a company)
  • do research (research is uncountable, so not "a research")
  • do a job / a task / your work
  • do overtime
  • do a course / training
  • do the accounts / the paperwork / the admin
  • do someone a favour
  • do your best
  • do damage / harm (to a reputation, a relationship)
  • do well / badly (The new product is doing well.)

The mistakes I hear most

Several of the most common errors use neither "make" nor "do" in natural English. The right verb is something else completely.

What people sayNatural English
We need to do a decision today.We need to make a decision today.
Sorry, I did a mistake in the invoice.Sorry, I made a mistake on the invoice.
Can I make a question?Can I ask a question?
Let's make a meeting next week.Let's have a meeting next week. / Let's set up a meeting next week.
Let's make a pause.Let's take a break.
I made the exam in June.I took (or sat) the exam in June.
Can you make a photo of the damage?Can you take a photo of the damage?
We're making a party on Friday.We're having a party on Friday.
I make sport three times a week.I do sport three times a week. / I play football…

"Sit an exam" is British. "Take an exam" works everywhere. Note also "make it to the meeting", which is correct but means something different: manage to attend. "Sorry, I can't make it on Thursday" is a very natural way to decline.

Grey areas

Some pairs accept both verbs, and you don't need to worry about them.

  • Make a presentation / do a presentation / give a presentation. All are used. "Give" is the safest in formal writing. "Do" is common in speech.
  • Make a report / do a report. "Write a report" or "prepare a report" is clearer than both.
  • Do a deal / make a deal. Both are fine in British English.

When you aren't sure, a more specific verb is usually better anyway. "Prepare", "write", "run", "hold", "carry out" and "set up" all sound more precise than "make" or "do", especially in emails. "We carried out an audit" is stronger than "we did an audit".

A quick test

Fill each gap with the correct form of make, do, or another verb. Answers below.

  1. I've ___ some research on the three suppliers.

  2. We ___ a loss in the first quarter.

  3. Could you ___ me a favour and forward this to Anna?

  4. I'm sorry, I can't ___ it to the 3 o'clock call.

  5. Before we ___ a decision, can I ___ a question?

  6. The team has ___ a lot of progress this month.

Answers: 1 done, 2 made, 3 do, 4 make, 5 make / ask, 6 made.

How to make it stick

Reading a list doesn't change how you speak. Using the phrases does. So pick the three phrases from this page that you would actually say this week, probably "make a decision", "do research" and "make it to a meeting", and use each one in a real email or call. Then add three more. Grammar like this is learned in pairs of words, not rules.

If you mix these up in emails, you may also find email phrases that sound translated useful.
