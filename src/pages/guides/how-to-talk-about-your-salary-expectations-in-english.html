How to talk about your salary expectations in English

Research a realistic figure, give a range with your target near the bottom, and say it calmly in one or two sentences. Here are the exact phrases for each stage, from the first call to negotiating an offer.

Why this question feels hard in English

Talking about money is awkward in any language. In a second language it's worse, because you're also worried about sounding rude or greedy. The good news is that British hiring conversations about salary are usually quite polite and indirect, and a few set phrases cover almost everything.

Before the interview: know your number

  • Check the job advert. Many UK adverts show a salary range. If there is one, your answer should sit inside it.
  • Look at similar roles in the same city and sector on job boards.
  • Know the format. In the UK, salaries are normally discussed as an annual figure before tax, for example "forty-five thousand a year". Some other countries talk about monthly pay, so convert before the interview, not during it.
  • Decide three numbers: your ideal, your realistic target, and your minimum. Keep the minimum to yourself.

Stage 1: early in the process

Recruiters often ask in the first call. If you'd rather not give a number yet, you can politely move it on:

  • "I'd like to understand the role a bit better first. Could you tell me the budgeted range?"
  • "I'm flexible. What range did you have in mind for this role?"
  • "I'm sure we can agree on something fair if it's the right fit."

If they insist, give a range. Refusing twice starts to sound evasive.

Stage 2: giving a range

The standard answer is a range, with a short reason:

"Based on my experience and what I've seen for similar roles in Manchester, I'm looking for something in the range of forty-five to fifty thousand."

Two tips teachers know from hearing this answer many times:

  • Put your real target at the bottom of the range. Employers tend to hear the lower number. If you'd be happy with 45, say "45 to 50", not "40 to 45".
  • Stop talking after the number. Nervous candidates keep going: "…but I'm flexible, and of course it depends, and I could accept less…". You've just negotiated against yourself. Say the range, then wait.

Stage 3: if they ask about your current salary

You don't have to share it. You can redirect to what you're looking for:

"I'd rather focus on what's right for this role. Based on the responsibilities, I'm looking for around forty-eight thousand."

If you do share it, mention the whole package: "My base salary is forty thousand, plus an annual bonus and private healthcare."

Stage 4: responding to an offer

Never accept or reject on the spot. Thank them and ask for time:

  • "Thank you, I'm really pleased to receive the offer. Could I have a day or two to think it over?"
  • "Could you send the details in writing so I can look at the full package?"

If you want to ask for more, be positive first, then specific:

"I'm very excited about the role and I'd like to make this work. Based on the scope of the job, I was hoping for something closer to fifty thousand. Is there any flexibility on the base salary?"

If salary is fixed, you can ask about other things: "If the salary can't move, would there be flexibility on the start date, holiday, or a review after six months?" For more on this, see negotiation phrases in English.

Phrases that sound too direct

AvoidSay instead
I want 50,000.I'm looking for something around 50,000.
This offer is too low.I was hoping for something a little higher.
I will not accept less than 45.For me to move, I'd need to be at around 45.
Give me more money and I'll accept.If we could get closer to 50, I'd be happy to accept.

The meaning hasn't changed, but the second column sounds like a professional discussion rather than a demand. Softening words like "around", "a little" and "I was hoping" do a lot of work in British English. There's more on this in how to sound less direct (and less rude) in English.

Saying numbers clearly

Small errors with numbers cause real confusion here, so practise saying yours aloud:

  • £45,000 = "forty-five thousand" (not "forty-five thousands")
  • £47,500 = "forty-seven and a half thousand" or "forty-seven thousand five hundred"
  • "45k" is common in writing and casual speech: "forty-five k".
  • "Per annum" appears in adverts, but in speech say "a year".
  • "Gross" means before tax, "net" or "take-home" means after.
