IELTS Writing Task 1 Academic: describing graphs clearly

Task 1 isn't about describing every number. It's about choosing the important ones, grouping them, and writing a clear overview. That last part is what most often separates band 6 from band 7.

What the task involves

In Academic Writing Task 1 you describe visual information in your own words: one or more graphs, charts or tables, or a diagram of an object, device, process or event. You must write at least 150 words, and you should spend about 20 minutes on it. Task 2 counts for twice as much as Task 1, so don't let Task 1 eat into your essay time.

Source: IELTS Academic test format in detail on ielts.org. Check it before your exam in case anything has changed.

Why the overview matters so much

The official band descriptors for Task Achievement are very specific here. At band 7, the response "presents a clear overview, the data are appropriately categorised, and main trends or differences are identified". At band 6, "a relevant overview is attempted". So the overview isn't optional decoration. It's a large part of the difference between the two bands.

An overview is two or three sentences that describe the big picture without any numbers. If someone could only read one paragraph of your answer, the overview is the one that should tell them what the graph shows.

A four-paragraph plan

  1. Introduction (1 sentence). Paraphrase what the graph shows: what, where, when.

  2. Overview (2 sentences). The main trend and the biggest difference. No figures.

  3. Details 1 (3–4 sentences). One group of data, with key figures.

  4. Details 2 (3–4 sentences). The other group, with key figures and comparisons.

There's no need for a conclusion. The overview already does that job, and a separate conclusion usually just repeats it.

Finding the overview in two minutes

Before you write, ask yourself three questions about the graph:

  • What's the general direction? Did most things go up, go down, or stay level?
  • What's the biggest or smallest? Which category was highest throughout, or at the end?
  • What changed places? Did one line overtake another?

Write the answers as two sentences and you have your overview. For example: "Overall, the use of trains rose steadily over the period, while car use fell. By 2020, trains had overtaken cars as the most common way of commuting."

Grouping the data

"Appropriately categorised" in the descriptor means your body paragraphs should group the information logically rather than walking through it item by item. Common ways to group:

  • Rising versus falling categories.
  • Before and after a turning point, such as a peak.
  • Largest versus smallest categories.
  • For two charts, one chart per paragraph, with comparisons between them.

A line graph with six lines doesn't need six paragraphs. It needs two well-chosen groups.

Language for trends

Vary the grammar, not just the vocabulary. You can describe the same change in two ways:

  • Verb and adverb: "Sales rose sharply between 2015 and 2018."
  • Adjective and noun: "There was a sharp rise in sales between 2015 and 2018."

For the size of the change: slightly, gradually, steadily, significantly, sharply, dramatically. Use "dramatically" only for changes that really are dramatic. Examiners notice when the words don't match the picture.

Language for comparisons and figures

  • "Car use was almost twice as high as bus use."
  • "Spending on food was considerably higher than spending on transport."
  • "Just under a third of respondents…" / "Roughly half…"
  • "The figure for Germany, at 42%, was the highest."
  • "…compared with only 12% in Spain."

Approximations such as "just over half" or "roughly a quarter" make your writing read more naturally than listing exact numbers in every sentence.

Mistakes I correct most often

  • Giving opinions or reasons. "Car use fell because people care about the environment" is a guess. Task 1 describes what the data shows. It doesn't explain why.
  • Describing every number. Choose the figures that support your main points.
  • Wrong tense. Past data takes the past simple. Projections take "is expected to" or "is predicted to".
  • "The number of" versus "the amount of". Use "number" with countable things (the number of cars) and "amount" with uncountable ones (the amount of energy).
  • No overview. Still the most common problem, and the most expensive.
