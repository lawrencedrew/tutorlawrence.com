The STAR method for interview answers, with examples

STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. It turns "Tell me about a time when…" questions into short, clear stories. Here's how to use it in English, with two full examples and the grammar that holds it together.

When you need it

Many UK and international employers use competency-based questions. You'll recognise them by the opening:

  • "Tell me about a time when you…"
  • "Give me an example of…"
  • "Describe a situation where…"
  • "Have you ever had to…?"

The interviewer wants evidence, not opinions. "I'm good under pressure" is an opinion. A story about the week your supplier went bust is evidence.

The four parts, and how long each should be

PartWhat you sayRough share
SituationWhere, when, what the background wasShort: 1–2 sentences
TaskWhat you had to do, or what the problem wasShort: 1 sentence
ActionWhat you did, step by stepMost of the answer
ResultWhat happened, ideally with a number, and what you learnt1–2 sentences

The most common problem I hear is a long Situation and a tiny Action. People spend a minute explaining the company structure, then say "so I fixed it" and stop. Flip that. The interviewer is scoring the Action.

Example 1: "Tell me about a time you dealt with a difficult deadline."

Situation: "Last year, our biggest client brought forward the launch of a new product by three weeks."

Task: "I was responsible for getting all the packaging artwork approved and printed in time."

Action: "First, I listed every step and worked out which ones we could run at the same time instead of one after another. Then I called the printer and asked them to reserve a slot for us before the artwork was final, so we wouldn't lose time in their queue. I also set up a short daily call with the client's marketing team, so approvals came back in hours instead of days."

Result: "We delivered two days early. The client used the same process for their next launch, and I learnt that most delays come from waiting, not from the work itself."

Example 2: "Give me an example of a disagreement with a colleague."

Situation: "In my previous role, a colleague in sales kept promising customers delivery dates our warehouse couldn't meet."

Task: "I needed to stop the complaints without damaging our working relationship."

Action: "Rather than raising it in a team meeting, I asked him for a coffee and showed him the delivery data. It turned out he didn't have access to our stock levels. So I set up a simple shared sheet that showed realistic dates for each product, and I checked in with him after a week to see if it was working."

Result: "Complaints about late deliveries dropped noticeably over the next quarter, and he started asking me to check dates before big deals."

Notice that the result doesn't invent a percentage. If you have a real number, use it. If you don't, describe the change honestly. Interviewers often ask "How did you measure that?", and a made-up figure falls apart quickly.

The grammar that makes STAR sound fluent

  • Past simple for the main events: "I called", "I set up", "we delivered". This is the backbone of the story.
  • Past continuous for background: "We were launching a new product when…"
  • Past perfect for things that happened earlier: "The client had already announced the date, so we couldn't move it."
  • Sequencing words: first, then, after that, at the same time, in the end.
  • Present simple for the lesson: "I learnt that most delays come from waiting." The lesson is still true, so it stays in the present.

Say "I", not "we"

Many people, especially from cultures where talking about yourself feels like boasting, say "we" all the way through. The interviewer then can't tell what you did. Use "we" for the team's situation and "I" for your actions:

Too vagueClear
We analysed the data and we found the problem.I went through six months of order data and found that most errors came from one form.
We decided to change the process.I suggested a change to the process, and my manager approved it.

It isn't arrogant in a British interview. It's what they need to hear.

Prepare five stories, not fifty

You don't need a story for every possible question. Prepare five strong ones that cover different skills: a deadline, a conflict, a mistake you fixed, a time you led something, and a time you improved a process. One good story can answer several questions with a different emphasis. Write each one in four short lines, one per letter, and practise telling it out loud in under two minutes.
