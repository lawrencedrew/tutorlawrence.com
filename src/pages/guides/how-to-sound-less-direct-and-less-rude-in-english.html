How to sound less direct (and less rude) in English

A sentence can be grammatically perfect and still sound rude to a British colleague. The fix is usually a small softening word or a question instead of an instruction. Here are the patterns that make the difference.

Why your English might sound too direct

In many languages, being direct is efficient and respectful. You say what you need and the other person appreciates the clarity. British English works differently at work. Requests, disagreement and criticism are usually wrapped in softer language, and when that wrapping is missing, the listener notices.

The tricky part is that nobody will tell you. A British colleague won't say "That was rude." They will just be a little cooler with you, and you won't know why. In lessons, this is one of the most common things I work on with strong speakers, because their grammar is already fine.

Requests: ask, don't tell

The simplest change is to turn an instruction into a question. Even with "please", an imperative can sound like an order from a manager.

Too directSofter
Send me the report.Could you send me the report?
Please check this today.Would you be able to check this today?
I want a meeting with you.Could we find time for a quick meeting?
You must update the file.Could you update the file when you get a chance?
Call me.Would you mind giving me a call?

For a bigger favour or a more senior person, go one step further: I was wondering if you could… or Would it be possible to…? The past tense in "I was wondering" makes it sound less demanding, not because it happened in the past.

Disagreeing without starting an argument

"You're wrong" or "That's not correct" are very strong in British English. Most disagreement comes with a cushion in front of it.

Too directSofter
You're wrong.I'm not sure I agree.
That's a bad idea.I can see some problems with that.
No, it won't work.I'm not sure that would work, because…
That's not true.I think the figures say something slightly different.

Another useful pattern is a question: Have you thought about…? Wouldn't it be quicker to…? A negative question invites the other person to agree, rather than telling them they're wrong. There's more on this in how to disagree politely in English.

Five small words that soften anything

  • a bit / slightly: The price is a bit high. It's slightly later than we planned.
  • just: I just wanted to check… Just a quick question…
  • might / could: It might be better to wait. That could be a problem.
  • I think / I feel: I think we need more time.
  • perhaps / maybe: Perhaps we could look at it again next week.

None of these change your message. They change how it lands. "The price is high" is a complaint. "The price is a bit higher than we expected" is the start of a negotiation.

Bad news and saying no

"No" on its own is rarely used at work. Instead:

  • I'm afraid that won't be possible.
  • Unfortunately, we can't do that this month.
  • I'd love to help, but I'm completely full this week.

"I'm afraid" here has nothing to do with fear. It simply signals that bad news is coming, and it's one of the most useful phrases in British English. For emails, see how to say no politely in a business email.

"You" and "we"

"You didn't send the figures" sounds like an accusation. "The figures didn't arrive" or "I don't think we've got the figures yet" describe the same problem without pointing at a person. Similarly, "We need to fix this" sounds like teamwork, and "You need to fix this" sounds like blame. Use "you" carefully when something has gone wrong.

A before and after

Before: "Hi Tom, the presentation has mistakes. You must correct slide 5 and send it to me today. The numbers are wrong."

After: "Hi Tom, thanks for the presentation. I think there might be a small issue on slide 5: the numbers don't seem to match the report. Could you have a look and send me an updated version today if possible?"

Both ask for the same thing by the same deadline. Only one of them will get a friendly reply.

Don't soften too much

One warning. Softening is about tone, not about hiding the message. If you need something today, say "today". If something is a serious problem, say "This is a serious problem" and soften the sentence around it instead. Some learners discover softening and then use so much of it that nobody understands what they're asking for. Keep the request or the deadline clear, and make everything around it gentle.

It also helps to understand how British colleagues soften their own messages, because they mean more than they say. See understanding British understatement at work.
