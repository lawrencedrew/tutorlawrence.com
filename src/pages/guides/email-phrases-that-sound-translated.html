Email phrases that sound translated, and what to write instead

Your email is grammatically correct, so why does it sound strange? Usually one phrase has been translated straight from your own language. Here are the ones I correct most often.

Eight phrases and their natural versions

Sounds translatedWhat a British colleague would write
I'm writing you to inform that…I'm writing to let you know that…
Please find in attachment the report.I've attached the report.
We will discuss about it on Monday.We'll discuss it on Monday.
I remain at your disposal.Let me know if you have any questions.
Waiting for your answer.Could you let me know by Friday?
Kindly ask you to send…Could you send…?
I would like to ask you if it is possible…Would it be possible to…?
Thank you in advance for your cooperation.Thanks for your help with this.

Why the second version works better

British business English has moved towards short, friendly and direct, while staying polite. The translated versions are often more formal than the situation needs, and formal can read as cold or even irritated.

A few patterns sit behind most of the fixes:

  • Use contractions. "I've attached", "we'll discuss" and "I'm writing" are normal in work emails. They aren't too casual.
  • Ask a question instead of stating a wish. "Could you send…?" is politer than "I want you to send" and shorter than "I would like to ask you if…".
  • Give a deadline instead of waiting. "Waiting for your answer" sounds impatient. "Could you let me know by Friday?" is polite and gets a reply.
  • Watch the prepositions. In English you discuss something, not "discuss about" it. "Write to you", not "write you", is the British form.

A quick before and after

Before: "Dear Mr Smith, I am writing you to inform that the delivery will be late. Please find in attachment the new schedule. Waiting for your answer. I remain at your disposal."

After: "Hi John, I'm writing to let you know the delivery will be two days late. I've attached the new schedule. Could you confirm it works for you by Thursday? Let me know if you have any questions."

Same information, half the stiffness, and a much clearer request.

How to stop doing it

Pick the two phrases from the table you use most and write the new versions on a sticky note by your screen. After a couple of weeks of using them, you'll stop reaching for the old ones.
