How to chase an unpaid invoice in English

Chasing payment works best in stages: friendly first, firmer second, formal last. Each reminder should include the invoice number, the amount, the due date and a clear next step.

Before you write: the four details every reminder needs

  • Invoice number, so they can find it in their system.
  • Amount and currency: €2,450, not "the payment".
  • Original due date.
  • A copy of the invoice attached, every time.

The person who reads your email is often in accounts, not the person who ordered from you. They may never have seen the invoice. Make it easy for them to pay without asking a single question.

Stage 1: the friendly reminder

Send this a few days after the due date. Assume it's an oversight. It usually is.

"Subject: Invoice 1043, due 28 February

Hi Sophie,

I hope you're well. Just a quick reminder that invoice 1043 for €2,450 was due on 28 February. I've attached a copy for reference.

Could you let me know when we can expect payment? If it's already been sent, please ignore this email, and thank you.

Best regards,
Andrei"

"If it's already been sent, please ignore this email" is an important line. It gives the reader a polite way out, and it protects you if the payment and your email crossed.

Other friendly openings:

  • I'm just checking on the status of invoice 1043.
  • I may have missed it, but I don't think we've received payment for…
  • Just a gentle reminder that…

Stage 2: the firmer reminder

If there's no reply or payment after a week or two, be more direct. Still polite, but no more "just" and "gentle".

"Subject: Overdue: invoice 1043 (€2,450)

Hi Sophie,

I'm following up on invoice 1043 for €2,450, which is now two weeks overdue. I haven't had a reply to my email of 3 March.

Could you please arrange payment by Friday 21 March, or let me know if there's a problem with the invoice?

I've attached the invoice again, along with our bank details.

Kind regards,
Andrei"

Asking "is there a problem with the invoice?" is useful. Sometimes payment is stuck because of a missing purchase order number or a wrong address. This question brings that out.

Stage 3: the final notice

Now the tone becomes formal. State the facts, the deadline and what you'll do next. Only mention a step you're really prepared to take.

"Subject: Final notice: invoice 1043 (€2,450), overdue since 28 February

Dear Ms Laurent,

Despite our reminders of 3 March and 14 March, invoice 1043 for €2,450 remains unpaid. It is now over four weeks overdue.

Please arrange payment in full by Friday 4 April. If we don't receive payment by then, we will have to pause further work on your account and pass the matter to our finance department.

If you've already made the payment, please send us the remittance details.

Yours sincerely,
Andrei Popescu"

Notice the change from "Hi Sophie" to "Dear Ms Laurent", and from "Best regards" to "Yours sincerely". In English, a sudden rise in formality is itself a signal. The reader will understand that things are serious without you needing to sound angry.

If you're thinking of charging interest or using a collection agency, check your contract and the law that applies in your country first. The rules differ, and a threat you can't carry out weakens your position.

Words that cause confusion

  • "Overdue" means the due date has passed. "Outstanding" means unpaid, whether late or not.
  • "Remittance" is the confirmation that a payment has been sent. "Could you send the remittance advice?" is a normal request.
  • "Settle" an invoice means pay it. "Could you settle this by Friday?" is common in British business English.
  • "Pay the invoice", not "pay for the invoice". You pay for goods, but you pay an invoice or a bill.

What I tell students who hate chasing money

Many people feel embarrassed asking for money in a second language, so they write long, apologetic emails. Don't. "Sorry to bother you, I know you're very busy, I hate to ask, but…" makes the request look optional. Asking to be paid for work you've done is normal business. Short and factual is polite enough. Save the warmth for one line, like "Thanks for your help with this", at the end.
