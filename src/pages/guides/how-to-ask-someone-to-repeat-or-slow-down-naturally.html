How to ask someone to repeat or slow down, naturally

Asking someone to repeat isn't a sign of weak English. Native speakers do it constantly. The trick is to ask about the specific part you missed, so you sound engaged rather than lost.

The problem with "Sorry?" and "What?"

Most people learn one phrase for this, "Can you repeat, please?", and use it for everything. It works, but it has two problems. It sounds a little like a classroom, and it asks the speaker to repeat the whole thing, which they'll do at exactly the same speed.

"What?" on its own is too blunt in a work context. "Pardon?" is polite but can sound slightly old-fashioned to some people. "Sorry?" with a rising tone is the everyday British choice, and it's fine for a single missed word. For anything longer, you need something more precise.

Ask about the part you missed

This is the single most useful habit. Instead of asking for everything again, repeat the part you did understand and ask about the rest.

  • "Sorry, you said the meeting's moved to when?"
  • "Sorry, the report needs to go to who?"
  • "You lost me after 'the budget'. Could you go over that again?"
  • "Sorry, what was the name of the supplier?"

This shows you were listening, it saves time, and the speaker will naturally say the missing part more slowly and clearly, because you've pointed at it.

SituationPhrase
Missed one word"Sorry?" / "Sorry, what was that?"
Missed a sentence"Sorry, I didn't catch that. Could you say it again?"
Missed the end"Sorry, I missed the last part."
Formal or senior person"I'm sorry, would you mind repeating that?"
Long explanation"Could you run that past me one more time?"

"I didn't catch that" is the phrase to learn first. It puts the problem on hearing, not understanding, so it never sounds like a confession that your English isn't good enough.

Asking someone to slow down

This feels more uncomfortable, because it seems to criticise the speaker. Make it about the information, not about them.

  • "Sorry, could you slow down a little? I want to make sure I get all the details."
  • "Could you take that a bit more slowly? I'm writing it down."
  • "Sorry, there's a lot here. Could we go through it step by step?"
  • "Do you mind going over the numbers again, slowly?"

"I'm writing it down" is a great reason to give. It's true, it sounds professional, and nobody can be offended by it.

When you heard the words but didn't understand

Sometimes the issue isn't speed. It's an unfamiliar word, an idiom or an acronym. Ask directly. People explain gladly.

  • "Sorry, what does 'EOD' stand for?"
  • "I'm not familiar with that expression. What does it mean?"
  • "When you say 'let's take it offline', do you mean after the meeting?"
  • "Could you give me an example?"

Checking your understanding by putting it in your own words is even better: "So, just to check: you want the draft by Thursday, and the final version next week?" If you're right, they say yes. If not, they correct you, and nobody loses time.

Phrases to avoid

AvoidSay instead
Repeat, please.Sorry, could you say that again?
Speak slower.Could you slow down a little?
I don't understand your English.Sorry, I didn't quite follow that.
What did you say?Sorry, what was that?

The first column isn't wrong grammatically. It just sounds like an order, because it has no softening. "Could you…" and "Sorry" do almost all the work.

What if you have to ask twice?

It happens, especially with strong accents. Don't pretend the second time. Change your approach instead:

  • "Sorry, I'm still not getting it. Could you type it in the chat?"
  • "Would you mind spelling that for me?"
  • "Could you send me a quick email with the details, so I've got them right?"

Moving to writing is completely normal on calls and much better than guessing. On video calls with a bad connection, the phrases in phone and video calls: phrases for when the line is bad will help too.

A tip from lessons: ask early

Students often wait too long. They miss one sentence, then hope the next one will explain it. By the third sentence they're completely lost and too embarrassed to ask. Ask the first time you miss something important. One small "Sorry, you said when?" at the start is easy. "Sorry, I didn't understand the last five minutes" is not.
