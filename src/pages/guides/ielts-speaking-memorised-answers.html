Why memorised answers lose marks in IELTS Speaking

Learning answers by heart feels like the safe way to prepare. In the Speaking test it's usually the fastest way to lose a band.

What the examiner is listening for

IELTS Speaking is a face-to-face conversation of 11 to 14 minutes in three parts. You're marked on four things, equally: fluency and coherence, vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation. The examiner is judging how you use English when you have to think, not how well you can recite.

Examiners are trained to recognise rehearsed answers. Language that is clearly memorised doesn't count as evidence of your level, so a polished script can earn less than a natural answer with a few mistakes.

How memorised answers give themselves away

  • Your fluency suddenly changes. You hesitate on the small talk, then deliver a perfect paragraph at speed. The contrast is obvious.
  • The answer doesn't quite fit the question. A prepared answer about "your hometown" sounds odd when the question was about how it has changed.
  • The vocabulary is too impressive for the rest of your speech. Rare idioms next to basic grammar mistakes stand out.
  • You lose your place. When the examiner interrupts or moves on, a memorised answer has nowhere to go.

What to prepare instead

Ideas, not scripts. Have three or four real stories from your life that you can tell in English: a trip, a difficult day at work, a person who influenced you, something you learned. Most Part 2 cards can be bent to fit one of them.

A simple answer shape. Answer the actual question in your first sentence, give a reason, then an example. "Yes, quite a lot. Mainly because… For example, last year…" It keeps you relevant and gives the examiner what they need.

Natural thinking time. If you need a second, say so in English: "That's an interesting question, I haven't really thought about it, but…" That counts as fluent. Silence and "ehhh" don't.

Practise the way the test works

Get someone to ask you questions you haven't seen, and answer straight away. Record yourself, listen back, and note the two or three mistakes that keep coming up. Fix those, then repeat with new questions. That's what moves a score.

Natural and slightly imperfect scores higher than perfect and rehearsed.
