Ten IELTS Writing mistakes I mark every week

Most lost marks in IELTS Writing come from the same small set of habits, not from a lack of English. Here are the ten I correct most often, what each one costs you, and how to fix it.

First, how the marks work

Each task is marked on four equally weighted criteria: Task Achievement (Task 1) or Task Response (Task 2), Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy. Task 1 needs at least 150 words, Task 2 at least 250, and Task 2 counts twice as much as Task 1. Keep that in mind as you read: a mistake in Task 2 costs double.

Source: IELTS, IELTS scoring in detail and test format in detail. Check them before your exam in case anything has changed.

Mistakes 1–3: answering the wrong question

1. Writing about the topic, not the question. The question is "Do the advantages of working from home outweigh the disadvantages?" The essay lists advantages, lists disadvantages, and never says which outweighs which. That's a Task Response problem, and it's the most expensive mistake on this list.

Fix: underline the instruction words before you plan ("outweigh", "to what extent", "discuss both views and give your opinion"). Your conclusion must answer that exact instruction.

2. No clear position. "There are many opinions on this issue and both sides have valid points." The examiner still doesn't know what you think.

Fix: state your view in the introduction and repeat it in the conclusion. "I believe the benefits are greater, mainly because…"

3. Task 1 with no overview. In Academic Task 1, candidates describe every number and never say what the chart shows overall.

Fix: write a two-sentence overview of the main trends, with no figures. "Overall, car use rose steadily while rail travel fell, and by 2020 cars were the most common way to commute." See describing graphs clearly for more.

Mistakes 4–6: structure and linking

4. A memorised introduction. "In this day and age, it is a controversial issue that has sparked heated debate." Examiners see this sentence constantly, and it tells them nothing about your English.

Fix: paraphrase the question in one sentence, then give your position. Two sentences is enough.

5. Linking words in every sentence. "Firstly… Moreover… Furthermore… In addition… Nevertheless…" Using a linker at the start of every sentence is mechanical, and it can lower your Coherence and Cohesion score rather than raise it.

Fix: use fewer, and link with reference words too: "this problem", "such policies", "these figures". My guide to linking words examiners reward goes deeper.

6. One idea per paragraph, never developed. A paragraph with a main idea and no explanation or example reads like a list.

Fix: idea, then why, then an example or result. Three or four sentences, each one doing a job.

Mistakes 7–9: vocabulary and grammar

What I markWhat to write instead
The government should invest more money for education.The government should invest more money in education.
People are more and more depending on technology.People increasingly depend on technology.
It has a lot of advantages and a lot of disadvantages.It has clear benefits, but also some serious drawbacks.
Nowadays, the technology is changing the society.Technology is changing society.
This can give a big damage to the environment.This can do serious damage to the environment.

7. Wrong collocations. "Give damage", "make a crime", "invest for". The word is right; the partner word is wrong. Collocation is part of Lexical Resource, so learn words in pairs, not alone.

8. Articles with general nouns. "The technology", "the society", "the education" when you mean them in general. In English, uncountable and plural nouns used in a general sense usually take no article.

9. "Big words" used wrongly. Students reach for "plethora", "detrimental" and "paramount" to impress. An advanced word used incorrectly is still an error. A precise ordinary word scores better than an impressive wrong one.

Mistake 10: no time left to check

The last mistake causes many of the others. Candidates write until the final second and never reread. Then simple errors stay in: missing plural -s, "it's" for "its", a sentence that started one way and finished another.

Fix: plan your 60 minutes before the test, not during it. The official advice is about 20 minutes for Task 1 and 40 for Task 2. I suggest doing Task 2 first, because it's worth more, and saving three minutes at the end of each task to read it once, looking only for your personal top three errors.

How to use this list

  1. Take your last two essays and check them against all ten points.

  2. Find your top three. Nearly everyone has a "favourite" mistake that appears in every essay.

  3. Write them at the top of every practice essay and check for them before you finish.

  4. After two weeks, replace any you've stopped making.

That small habit does more than learning a hundred new words, because it removes the errors the examiner sees over and over in your writing.
