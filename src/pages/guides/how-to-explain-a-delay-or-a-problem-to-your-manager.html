How to explain a delay or a problem to your manager

Bad news goes down best when it's early, short and comes with a plan. Say what's happened, why it matters, what you're doing about it, and what you need. Four parts, in that order.

Why it goes wrong in a second language

When you're nervous, two things tend to happen. Either you start with a long explanation of everything that went wrong, and your manager waits a minute and a half before hearing the actual problem. Or you soften it so much that they don't realise anything is wrong at all.

Most managers care much less about the problem than about two other things: hearing it early, and seeing that you're in control of it. The language you use can show both.

The four-part structure

  1. The headline. One sentence. "The client report is going to be two days late."

  2. The reason. One or two sentences, factual, no blame. "We're still waiting for the sales figures from the Warsaw office."

  3. Your plan. "I've asked them to send the figures by Wednesday, and I'm finishing the other sections now, so it'll be ready on Friday."

  4. What you need, if anything. "Could you let the client know, or would you prefer me to?"

That whole message takes about twenty seconds to say. Your manager knows the problem, the cause, the new date and the decision they need to make.

Phrases for the headline

  • "I just wanted to give you a heads-up about…"
  • "I'm afraid we've run into a problem with…"
  • "There's been a delay with the delivery."
  • "I need to flag an issue with the budget."

"Give you a heads-up" means warn you in advance. It's friendly and very common in British offices. "Flag" means draw attention to something. Both show you're being proactive.

Phrases for the reason

  • "This is because…" / "It's due to…"
  • "We've had an unexpected problem with…"
  • "It's taken longer than expected because…"
  • "I underestimated how long the testing would take."

If it was your mistake, say so simply. "I underestimated the time needed" is honest and professional. A long apology isn't necessary, and blaming others, even when partly true, tends to make you look worse.

Phrases for your plan

  • "To fix this, I'm going to…"
  • "I've already spoken to IT, and they're looking into it."
  • "The new deadline would be Friday."
  • "There are two options. We could…, or we could…"

Offering two options is particularly effective. Your manager makes a quick decision instead of solving the problem for you.

Phrases for asking for help

  • "Would it be possible to get some extra help from the design team?"
  • "Could you speak to the client, or should I?"
  • "I'd appreciate your advice on how to handle this."

The last example matters. "Nothing serious" when it is actually serious will cost you trust later. Be accurate about size: "a minor issue", "a significant delay", "a serious problem" all mean different things, and your manager will take you at your word.

In an email

Use the same four parts, and put the headline in the subject line: "Client report: new date Friday 21st". Then:

"Hi Sarah, just a heads-up that the client report will be two days late. We're still waiting for the sales figures from Warsaw. I've asked for them by Wednesday and I'm finishing the other sections now, so the report will be with you on Friday. Would you like me to let the client know? Thanks, Piotr"

Five sentences. Nothing hidden, nothing dramatic. If the delay affects someone outside your team, the guide on how to apologise for a mistake in a work email covers what to send them.

Timing: tell them before they find out

The best phrase in this whole guide is "I wanted you to hear it from me first." It shows respect, and it turns a problem into a sign that you're reliable. Managers tend to remember who told them early, and who let them find out from someone else.

A tip from lessons: practise the first sentence

The hardest moment is the first sentence. Once it's out, the rest follows. Before the conversation, say your headline sentence out loud two or three times, exactly as you'll say it. When students do this before a real conversation, the explanation that follows is almost always calmer and shorter.
