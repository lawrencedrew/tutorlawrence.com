How to give feedback to a colleague in English

Good feedback in English is specific, about the work rather than the person, and ends with a way forward. Get those three things right, and even critical feedback will sound fair and friendly.

Why feedback is harder in a second language

In your own language, you soften criticism automatically, through tone, word choice and small jokes. In English, a lot of that disappears. What's left can sound much harsher than you meant. "Your report has many mistakes" might be a simple, honest statement in your language. In English, to a British colleague, it can feel like a telling-off.

The opposite also happens. Some people soften so much that the colleague doesn't realise there's a problem at all. The aim is the middle: clear, but kind.

A simple structure: what, why, what next

  1. What you noticed. Something specific and observable. "In yesterday's client call, the pricing slide came quite late."

  2. Why it matters. The effect, not your feelings about the person. "The client asked about cost twice before we got to it."

  3. What next. A suggestion or a question. "Next time, could we bring pricing forward? What do you think?"

Ending with a question turns criticism into a conversation. It also gives your colleague a chance to explain something you might not know.

Starting the conversation

  • "Have you got five minutes? I wanted to give you some quick feedback on the presentation."
  • "Can I share a couple of thoughts on the draft?"
  • "Do you mind if I make a suggestion?"

Asking permission first sounds polite, and it prepares the other person to listen. Choose a private moment for anything critical. Feedback in front of others, even mild, can embarrass people.

Positive feedback: be specific

"Good job" is nice, but it doesn't tell anyone what to repeat. Name the thing.

VagueSpecific
Good presentation.The way you explained the timeline with one slide was really clear.
Thanks for your work.Thanks for staying late to finish the figures. It meant we could send them on time.

Critical feedback: phrases that soften without hiding

  • "One thing I'd suggest is…"
  • "It might be worth…"
  • "I wonder if it would help to…"
  • "Something to think about for next time…"
  • "I noticed that… Was there a reason for that?"
  • "It would be even better if…"

"It would be even better if…" is a phrase I like a lot. It starts from the assumption that the work is already decent, and it points to one improvement.

Your report has many mistakes.I spotted a few errors in the figures on page 3. Could you double-check them before it goes out?

You talk too much in meetings.In the last meeting, a couple of people didn't get a chance to speak. Could you help me bring them in next time?

Notice the pattern: no "always" or "never", a specific example, and a question or request at the end. "Always" and "never" make people defensive immediately, because they can usually think of one exception.

The "feedback sandwich": use with care

You may have heard of putting criticism between two compliments. It can work, but many professionals recognise it, and if you use it every time, people start to ignore the compliments and wait for the "but". A cleaner approach: give positive feedback separately and often, so that when you do give critical feedback, it doesn't need hiding.

Feedback in writing

Written feedback loses your tone completely, so be extra careful. Lead with what works, number your suggestions so they look manageable, and close warmly: "Overall, it's in really good shape. Just a few small points below." For comments on documents, questions often work better than instructions: "Could we add a source here?" rather than "Add source." For more on softening, see how to sound less direct (and less rude) in English.

Receiving feedback

It works both ways. When someone gives you feedback, these phrases help:

  • "Thanks, that's really useful."
  • "That's fair. I'll keep that in mind."
  • "Could you give me an example, so I understand exactly what you mean?"

A tip from lessons: watch the word "feedback"

One small grammar point causes mistakes every week. "Feedback" is uncountable, so it's "some feedback" or "a piece of feedback", never "a feedback" or "feedbacks". The same goes for "advice" and "information". There's a full explanation in countable and uncountable nouns.
