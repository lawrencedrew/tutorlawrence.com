How to write a professional email in English

A professional email in English is short, friendly and makes its request obvious. Use the five-part structure below and most of your emails will be clearer than the ones you receive.

What "professional" means in English emails

Many of my students were taught that a professional email should be formal: long sentences, "I am writing to inform you", "I remain at your disposal". In British and international business today, that style usually reads as stiff or old-fashioned. Professional now means three things:

  • Clear. The reader knows why you wrote within the first two lines.
  • Polite. Requests are phrased as questions, and you say thank you.
  • Short. One topic per email, and paragraphs of one to three sentences.

Formality is a dial, not a rule. You turn it up for a first contact with a senior client and down for a colleague you message every day. The structure stays the same.

The five-part structure

  1. Subject line. Say what the email is about and, if needed, what you want. "Q3 budget: approval needed by Friday" beats "Question".

  2. Greeting. "Hi Anna," for most people you know. "Dear Ms Novak," for a first, formal contact. Add a comma, then start a new line.

  3. Purpose in one sentence. "I'm writing about the delivery schedule for March." or "Quick question about the Warsaw invoice."

  4. Details and the request. Give only what the reader needs to act. Put the request in its own short paragraph, ideally with a date.

  5. Close and sign-off. "Thanks," or "Best regards," and your name. One line of polite closing is enough, for example "Let me know if you have any questions."

Subject lines that get opened

Phrases for each part

Saying why you're writing

  • I'm writing about…
  • Just a quick note to let you know…
  • Following our call this morning, …
  • Thanks for your email about…

Making a request

  • Could you send me…?
  • Would it be possible to…?
  • Could you let me know by Wednesday?
  • I'd be grateful if you could… (more formal)

Giving information

  • I've attached the report.
  • Here are the main points:
  • Just to confirm, the meeting is on 4 March at 10am.

Closing

  • Let me know if you have any questions.
  • Thanks again for your help.
  • I look forward to hearing from you. (formal, fine for a first contact)

A full before and after

Before:

"Subject: Request. Dear Sir, I am writing to you in order to inform you that we have some problems with the last order and we would like to ask you if it would be possible that you check the quantities because in our opinion there are some differences with the invoice and we need this information urgently for our accounting department. Waiting for your answer. Best regards, Marta"

After:

"Subject: Order 4471: quantities don't match the invoice

Hi Tom,

I'm writing about order 4471, which arrived yesterday.

The quantities we received don't match the invoice. We got 40 boxes of item B12, but the invoice shows 50. I've attached a photo of the delivery note.

Could you check this and send a corrected invoice by Friday? Our accounts team needs it to close the month.

Thanks,
Marta"

The second version is about the same length, but the problem, the evidence, the request and the deadline each have their own place. Tom can reply in two minutes.

Five habits that make you sound more natural

  • Use contractions. "I'm", "I've", "don't" and "we'll" are normal in work emails, even with clients.
  • Put the request on its own line. If it's buried in the middle of a paragraph, people miss it.
  • Give a reason with a deadline. "by Friday, because the client meeting is on Monday" sounds reasonable. "ASAP" with no reason sounds pushy.
  • Cut "please find attached". "I've attached…" is shorter and more natural.
  • Read it once as the reader. Ask: if this were the only email I read today, would I know what to do?

The one thing a teacher notices

When I check students' emails, the grammar is rarely the main problem. The real problem is order. People write the background first and the request last, because that's polite in many languages. English readers skim, so the request at the bottom gets lost. Try writing the request sentence first, then add only the background the reader needs to say yes. It feels strange for a week. After that, your emails get faster replies.
