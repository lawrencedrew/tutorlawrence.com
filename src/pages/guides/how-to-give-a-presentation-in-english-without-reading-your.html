How to give a presentation in English without reading your slides

You read your slides because you're afraid of losing the words. The fix is to stop memorising sentences and start memorising the structure, then use a small set of signposting phrases to carry you between points.

Why reading feels safe but works against you

When you present in a second language, putting full sentences on the slides feels like a safety net. If your mind goes blank, the words are right there. The problem is that the audience can read faster than you can speak. Within a few seconds they've finished the slide and stopped listening to you. You also turn your back to them, your voice goes flat, and your English sounds less fluent than it really is.

In lessons, when students switch from reading to speaking from keywords, the difference is obvious immediately. They make a few more small mistakes, and they sound far more confident. Audiences forgive small mistakes. They don't forgive being bored.

Step 1: rebuild your slides around keywords

  1. One idea per slide. If a slide has two points, split it.

  2. A short headline, not a paragraph. "Costs up 12% since March" tells the story. You explain it.

  3. Three to five words per bullet, maximum. Bullets are prompts for you, not a script for the audience.

  4. Move the full sentences into your speaker notes, if you want them at all, and then cut those down too.

Step 2: memorise the skeleton, not the script

Learn three things by heart: your first two sentences, the order of your sections, and your final sentence. Everything else you say in your own words, from the keywords on the screen.

Memorising a whole script is a trap. If you forget one word, the next sentence goes too, because you remember it as a chain. If you remember ideas instead, you can always find a different way to say them.

Step 3: use signposting phrases

Signposting phrases tell the audience where you are. They also give you, the speaker, a fixed sentence to say while your brain catches up. Learn these and you'll never be stuck between slides.

StagePhrases
Opening"Thanks for having me. Today I want to talk about…" / "I'll keep this to about ten minutes."
Overview"I've divided this into three parts. First…, then…, and finally…"
Moving on"So, that's the background. Now let's look at…" / "Which brings me to…"
Pointing to a visual"As you can see here…" / "What this chart shows is…" / "The key number is this one."
Emphasising"The important thing here is…" / "If you remember one thing from today, it's this."
Summarising"So, to sum up…" / "Just to recap the main points…"
Closing"That's all from me. I'm happy to take any questions."

Step 4: describe, don't read

The real skill is turning a short bullet into a spoken sentence. Here's a slide bullet and two ways to deliver it.

Slide: "Delivery times: 5 days → 3 days"

Reading: "Delivery times. Five days to three days."

Speaking: "So, the big change is delivery. It used to take us five days. Now we're down to three, and that's mainly because of the new warehouse."

The spoken version uses simple English. It just adds the "so what": why the number matters.

Step 5: practise out loud, three times

Reading your presentation silently is not practice. Your mouth needs to have said the words. Here's a routine I give students before a real presentation:

  1. Run 1: talk through it with your notes, stopping whenever you need to. Notice where you get stuck.

  2. Run 2: fix those places. Usually they need a signposting phrase or a simpler sentence.

  3. Run 3: record yourself on your phone, start to finish, without stopping. Listen back once.

Three runs is usually enough for a ten-minute talk. More than five and you start to memorise a script by accident.

What to do if your mind goes blank

It happens to native speakers too. Have a rescue phrase ready:

  • "Let me just check my notes."
  • "So, where was I? Ah yes, the delivery times."
  • "Let me put that another way."

Then look at the keyword on your slide and start again with a simple sentence. A two-second pause feels long to you. To the audience it looks like you're thinking, which is fine.

Pronunciation: the words that matter most

Before the presentation, pick the five or six words you'll say most often, usually technical terms or product names, and check their stress. A wrongly stressed key word, repeated twenty times, is the thing most likely to confuse your audience. There's more on this in word stress: why native speakers misunderstand you.
