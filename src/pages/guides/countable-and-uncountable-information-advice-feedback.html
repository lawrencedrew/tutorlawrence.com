Countable and uncountable: information, advice, feedback

"Information", "advice" and "feedback" have no plural in English, so "informations" and "feedbacks" are always wrong. Here are the work nouns that behave like this, and how to count them when you need to.

What "uncountable" means in practice

An uncountable noun is treated as a mass, like water or sand, not as separate items. In practice that means four things:

  • no plural: information, never "informations"
  • no "a" or "an": some advice, not "an advice"
  • a singular verb: The equipment is new.
  • much and little, not many and few: How much feedback did you get?

The problem is that in many languages the same words are countable, so the plural feels completely natural. That's why even C1 speakers write "Thank you for the informations" in emails.

Feedback: the one people ask about most

"Feedback" deserves its own section, because it's everywhere at work and the countable form is so tempting. Here is what natural speakers actually say:

  • Thanks for the feedback. (the most common phrase of all)
  • Do you have any feedback on the draft?
  • I got some really useful feedback from the client.
  • I've got a couple of comments on slide 4. (when you want to count)
  • Just two small points on the report…

The practical insight: when you need to count, you rarely need "a piece of feedback". It's correct, but sounds a little formal. Native speakers switch to comments or points. The same goes for advice: "a piece of advice" is fine, but "a tip" or "a suggestion" is what you'll hear in a meeting. For more on giving it well, see how to give feedback to a colleague in English.

Nouns that are both

Some nouns change meaning depending on whether they are countable.

  • experience: She has a lot of experience in sales (knowledge from doing something, uncountable). The trip was a great experience (one event, countable).
  • work: I've got a lot of work (uncountable). The works of Shakespeare or roadworks are a different meaning.
  • business: Business is good this year (trade in general). She runs two businesses (companies).
  • time: I don't have much time (uncountable). I've called three times (occasions).
  • paper: The printer is out of paper (material). She wrote a paper on AI (a document).

Data and staff

Two words that cause debate. Data is technically the plural of "datum", so some scientific writing says "the data are". In everyday business English, "the data is" is normal and correct. Either is acceptable; just be consistent.

Staff means all the people who work for an organisation. In British English it usually takes a plural verb: The staff are very helpful. For one person, say a member of staff or an employee, never "a staff".

A habit that fixes this quickly

Before you send an email, search it for "informations", "advices", "feedbacks", "equipments" and "researches". If any appear, the fix is usually just deleting the "s". Then check the word before it: "many" becomes "a lot of", "a" becomes "some". It takes ten seconds, and after a couple of weeks you'll stop typing them in the first place.
